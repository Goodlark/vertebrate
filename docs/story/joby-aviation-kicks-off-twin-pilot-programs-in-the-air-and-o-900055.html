Joby Aviation Kicks Off Twin Pilot Programs, in the Air and on the Road

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-10

This week, Joby Aviation put two very different aircraft on two very different roads at once. In Fort Worth, Texas, the company began ferrying passengers on its first commercial eVTOL flights, a short hop between the city and Dallas-Fort Worth International Airport that turns a notoriously congested surface commute into a few minutes aloft. At the same time, some 1,500 miles of American highway away in spirit if not in fact, a modified Cessna Caravan retrofitted with Joby's autonomous flight systems set off on a ten-state road trip, the kind of barnstorming tour that once introduced small towns to the airplane itself, only this time the pilot is software.

Both efforts fall under the federal government's eIPP initiative, the White House-backed pilot program designed to fast-track real-world testing of advanced air mobility before the technology is fully baked into regulation. That a single company is running both threads simultaneously says something about where Joby thinks the money and the mandate actually are: not just in whisking commuters over freeway traffic, but in proving that autonomous aircraft can haul freight and respond to emergencies in the kind of unglamorous, high-stakes situations that rarely make it into a promotional video.

The Fort Worth flights matter because they are, in the driest bureaucratic sense, commercial — meaning fare-paying passengers, not test pilots in company jumpsuits, are the ones being carried across DFW's tarmac by battery power. Urban air mobility has spent nearly a decade as a slide-deck promise; this is the first time Joby has actually put a member of the public in the seat and charged for the privilege, a threshold that matters far more to regulators and investors than any number of demo-day flybys.

The Caravan's cross-country tour is the quieter but arguably more consequential story. Passenger eVTOLs solve a lifestyle problem — the same one Uber promised to fix with helicopters a decade ago and largely failed to. Autonomous cargo and emergency-response flights solve a supply-chain and public-safety problem, one with obvious appeal to rural hospitals, disaster-relief agencies, and logistics firms that don't care about noise complaints or scenic views, only about whether a package or a medevac patient arrives on time. By showing the same aircraft in ten different states, Joby is angling for a broader constituency than the well-heeled commuters of Fort Worth: state transportation officials, FAA regional offices, and the freight companies who will eventually decide whether autonomous cargo flights are worth insuring.

For the wider industry, the two-track approach is a tell. Joby, along with rivals like Archer and Beta, has long argued that eVTOLs will first prove themselves in short, low-risk routes before graduating to denser urban corridors. This week suggests a parallel track is emerging just as fast — one where the aircraft carries nothing more than data logs and freight, and the person most affected by its performance is a regulator watching from the ground, not a passenger watching from the window seat.
