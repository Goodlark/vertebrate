Tesla's Robotaxi Fleet Pulls Ahead of Waymo in Texas

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-11

Tesla's Robotaxi operation in Texas has pulled ahead of Waymo's, according to fleet data circulating this week, a reversal that would have seemed unlikely just months ago when Alphabet's self-driving unit held a commanding head start in the state. The shift, first reported by Teslarati, shows Tesla's small but growing fleet of modified Model Ys now outnumbering or outperforming Waymo's vehicles in the Austin market, where both companies have been racing to prove out commercial robotaxi service on public roads.

The milestone did not go unnoticed in Silicon Valley. Palmer Luckey, the Oculus founder and a prominent voice in tech circles, publicly endorsed Elon Musk's robotaxi ambitions, which carry a valuation tag of $150 billion attached to Tesla's autonomous-ride unit. "That's the bet," Luckey said, framing Tesla's Texas gains as early vindication of Musk's long-stated wager that camera-based, mass-manufactured autonomy would eventually outrun Waymo's more sensor-laden, geographically cautious approach.

It is worth pausing on just how unlikely this would have sounded a year ago. Waymo has spent the better part of a decade and billions of dollars building a reputation as the industry's most careful operator, layering lidar, radar, and cameras atop painstakingly mapped city grids before ever picking up a paying rider. Tesla, by contrast, has leaned on the same camera-only stack it ships in its consumer cars, betting that scale and iteration speed could substitute for Waymo's redundancy. Austin has become the proving ground for that argument, and for now, at least by fleet-size metrics, Tesla is winning it.

The stakes extend well beyond bragging rights in one Texas city. Robotaxis represent the clearest near-term path for autonomous vehicle companies to generate real revenue, rather than research-grade demonstrations, and whichever approach wins the public's trust first could set the template the rest of the industry follows. For Tesla, a Texas lead offers fresh ammunition for Musk's argument that the company's valuation should be judged less on car sales than on software and robotics — the thesis underlying that $150 billion figure. For Waymo, ceding ground in even one market raises uncomfortable questions about whether its cautious, capital-intensive model can scale fast enough against a rival willing to move faster and spend less per vehicle.

For riders and city regulators, the competition is likely to mean more robotaxis showing up on Austin streets sooner, and a faster, messier real-world test of which safety philosophy actually holds up outside a controlled pilot. The economic stakes are not abstract either: autonomous ride-hailing is one of the few robotics bets with a plausible multibillion-dollar consumer market attached, and Tesla's Texas numbers suggest that race now has a genuine second contender, not just an also-ran chasing Waymo's taillights.
