Waymo Pushes Further Into the Bay Area and Beyond, But the Math Still Doesn't Work

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-29

Waymo opened its robotaxi service to riders in San Jose, Mountain View, Sunnyvale and other pockets of the South Bay this week, pushing its driverless footprint deeper into the region that birthed the technology in the first place. The expansion, confirmed in filings and reported by NBC Los Angeles, extends a service area that already covers San Francisco, the Peninsula and parts of the East Bay, and follows a string of announcements that have sent Waymo cars into Los Angeles, Phoenix, Austin and, this year, across the Atlantic to London and Tokyo pilots. The company, a unit of Alphabet, now says it logs more than 200,000 paid rides a week across its U.S. markets.

On paper, the map keeps filling in. In practice, as CNBC reported, the expansion is doing more to project momentum than to change the economics underneath it. Waymo remains a sliver of a sliver: even in San Francisco, where it has operated longest and most visibly, it captures a small fraction of the rides that Uber and Lyft handle in the same city on the same day. Alphabet does not break out Waymo's financials in detail, but the company has long been understood inside the Google ecosystem as a significant drain — the kind of moonshot that burns cash with no fixed date for turning a profit, propped up by a parent company that can afford to wait.

That patience is the real story here. Waymo's expansion strategy looks less like a business scaling toward profitability and more like a long, expensive demonstration — proof of technical capability stretched across more zip codes, intended to keep investors, regulators and rivals convinced that the company is winning a race that hasn't actually been run to its finish. Each new city requires its own mapping, sensor calibration, local regulatory sign-off and fleet of safety-monitored vehicles, costs that scale roughly linearly even as ridership in any one market stays thin.

For the robotics and autonomous-vehicle industry, the stakes are bigger than one company's balance sheet. Waymo is the furthest along of any serious driverless player, and its struggle to convert technical success into a profitable ride-hailing business is a signal to competitors, suppliers and the venture capital world about just how long the runway to real returns might be. For regulators and city planners in places like San Jose, each expansion is also a live experiment in traffic patterns, curb management and labor displacement that will shape policy long before the business itself turns a profit. And for riders, the proliferation of Waymo's white Jaguars is already reshaping daily commutes in a handful of neighborhoods — even if, for now, that reshaping is happening well ahead of any evidence that the company has found a way to make the ride pay for itself.
