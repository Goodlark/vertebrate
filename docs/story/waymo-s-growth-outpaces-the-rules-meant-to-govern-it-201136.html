Waymo's Growth Outpaces the Rules Meant to Govern It

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-22

Waymo is adding cities faster than Uber and Lyft drivers can refresh their earnings apps. The Alphabet-owned robotaxi company, which already shuttles paying passengers in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Austin, has been pushing into new metro areas at a pace that trade press now describes bluntly as a threat to human drivers at the ride-hailing giants that built the gig economy. Each new city is another market where a fleet of sensor-studded Jaguars can pick up fares without a driver who needs health insurance or a bathroom break.

That expansion has outrun something less visible than market share: the rulebook. In Colorado, a commentator writing in Colorado Newsline this week laid out an uncomfortable fact for state regulators — Waymo vehicles are operating, or poised to operate, on Colorado roads without a regulatory framework built specifically for them. The state, the argument goes, is "way behind," relying on patchwork traffic law and goodwill where it needs statute and enforcement.

Put the two stories side by side and a pattern emerges that is becoming familiar in American technology policy: deployment first, legislation later, if at all. Waymo's commercial logic is straightforward. Every city it enters without a dedicated regulatory apparatus is a city where it can scale on its own terms, setting precedent through presence rather than through permission. That is good business. It is also exactly the dynamic that has alarmed consumer advocates and labor organizers watching driverless cars multiply from a handful of pilot zones into a genuine transportation network.

The stakes are not abstract. For the drivers currently working for Uber and Lyft — a workforce that leans on gig income precisely because it offers flexibility unavailable in traditional employment — a robotaxi that undercuts fares in a new city is not a hypothetical disruption but a direct hit to take-home pay, arriving with no transition plan and no negotiating table. For riders, the appeal is obvious: a car that shows up, drives itself, and never asks for a tip. For city and state governments, the Colorado complaint points to a gap that matters well beyond one state — questions of liability in a crash, data collection on public streets, curb access, insurance requirements, and who exactly answers to regulators when the "driver" is a trunk full of lidar.

What makes this moment notable is not that a company is moving quickly — Silicon Valley has always moved quickly — but that the gap between deployment and governance is now wide enough to make the news twice in one week, once as a labor story and once as a policy failure. Autonomous vehicles are no longer a research curiosity confined to a few sunbelt test markets; they are a commercial reality spreading into states that have not yet decided what rules should apply to them. The robotaxi is already on the road. The traffic laws, in more places than Colorado, are still being written.
