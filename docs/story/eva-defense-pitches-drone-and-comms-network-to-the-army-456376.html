Eva Defense Pitches Drone-and-Comms Network to the Army

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-14

At the Association of the United States Army's annual conference this week, Eva Defense set up shop among the rows of camouflage-netted booths and PowerPoint generals to make a pitch that was less about hardware than about architecture. Company representatives met with Army officials and industry partners at AUSA 2026 to advance what Eva calls its autonomous drone and resilient communications programs — a phrase that, parsed carefully, suggests the company is less interested in selling the Army a single aircraft than in selling it a system.

That distinction is the point. Eva's own messaging, delivered under the banner "Eva Live," describes its drone as merely the opening move: the first visible piece of a network meant to stitch together aircraft, sensors, and communications infrastructure into something that behaves less like a product catalog and more like a nervous system. The drone flies, yes, but the company wants the Pentagon to think of it as a node — one terminal in a web that can pass targeting data, maintain links under jamming, and keep aircraft talking to each other even when the enemy is trying hard to make them stop.

This is, in defense-industry terms, a familiar ambition dressed in newer language. For decades the services have chased "networked" warfare, the idea that the real advantage isn't any single platform but the plumbing connecting them. What's changed is the urgency: Ukraine's battlefields have shown both how cheap drones can be devastating and how quickly communications links get contested or severed. An autonomous aircraft that goes silent and stupid the moment its signal is jammed is a liability, not an asset. A company that can promise resilience — connections that degrade gracefully rather than collapse — has something the Army's planners are, by all public accounts, hungry for.

Whether Eva Defense can deliver that promise at scale is an open question; AUSA meetings are the industry's version of speed dating, full of intent and short on signed contracts. But the positioning matters beyond this one company. It signals where the smaller, venture-backed entrants to defense tech think the real money and relevance lie: not in building a better single drone to compete with established primes, but in building the connective tissue that makes any drone — theirs or someone else's — useful in a contested, jammed, chaotic environment. If the Army buys that framing, it reshapes who gets a seat at the table, favoring firms selling systems and standards over those selling discrete boxes. For soldiers on some future battlefield, the difference between a drone that goes dark under jamming and one that reroutes around it isn't abstract. It's the difference between a tool and a casualty.
