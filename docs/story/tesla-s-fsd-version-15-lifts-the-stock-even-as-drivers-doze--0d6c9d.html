Tesla's FSD Version 15 Lifts the Stock Even as Drivers Doze Off

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-24

Tesla shares climbed this week as the company pushed out Full Self-Driving Version 15 to its fleet, a release that arrived alongside a broader rebound in tech stocks, according to Barron's. The timing was fortunate: investors have been waiting for a software update that might justify the premium they pay for Tesla's promise of autonomy, and Version 15 gave them something to cheer. The stock rose in step with the Nasdaq's gains, a reminder that Tesla still trades as much on narrative as on earnings.

But the narrative took a strange turn almost immediately. SlashGear reported that Tesla owners running the new FSD software are falling asleep at the wheel — not metaphorically, but literally nodding off while their cars pilot themselves down highways and surface streets. It's the kind of detail that would read as satire if it weren't being treated as a known behavioral pattern among drivers who have grown comfortable enough with the system to stop paying attention to it.

The irony is hard to miss. "Full Self-Driving" is, by Tesla's own legal and technical framing, not full self-driving at all — it remains a Level 2 driver-assistance system that requires a human to stay alert and ready to intervene. The software's name has always done more work than its capabilities, and now that gap is showing up in the most literal way possible: drivers trusting the "full" in the name more than the fine print that contradicts it.

For the robotics and autonomous-vehicle industry, this is a familiar and uncomfortable pattern. Systems that perform well enough to earn trust, but not well enough to deserve it, tend to produce exactly this kind of behavioral drift — what researchers have long called the "automation complacency" problem. The better a driver-assist system gets, the less a human is inclined to supervise it, even when the system still needs supervision. Tesla has iterated on FSD for years specifically to narrow that gap, but each version that feels smoother and more capable also risks deepening the very complacency that makes it dangerous.

The market, for its part, seems unbothered. Investors rewarded the release on the promise embedded in the version number — fifteen iterations deep, surely closer to the finish line — without much apparent weighting for the safety questions trailing behind it. That disconnect matters beyond Tesla's balance sheet. As more automakers chase their own driver-assistance milestones, the Version 15 story is a preview of the trade-off the whole industry will have to keep answering for: software can get better at driving long before humans get better at knowing when to stop trusting it.
