Waymo's Expansion Hits Speed Bumps From Santa Monica to D.C.

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-02

Waymo is having a rough month in the places where the rubber meets the road. In Santa Monica, the Alphabet-owned robotaxi company has filed an appeal against a city ban on overnight charging at two of its parking-lot stations, after local officials moved to restrict the hours its fleet can plug in near residential blocks. Three thousand miles east, in Washington, D.C., where Waymo has announced plans to launch service, ride-share drivers are already bracing for a competitor that doesn't need to sleep, eat, or take a cut for itself. Meanwhile, the company has been quietly importing thousands of Chinese-made Geely sedans to build out its fleet — a workaround, reporting shows, for tariffs that make American-assembled electric vehicles more expensive.

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Waymo has spent the past year pushing outward from its strongholds in Phoenix and San Francisco into Los Angeles, Austin, and now the nation's capital, adding cities at a pace that outstrips almost any other robotics deployment in America. The Santa Monica dispute is small in scale — two charging stations, a handful of overnight hours — but it's a preview of the zoning fights likely to recur as autonomous fleets need real estate: parking lots, curb space, charging infrastructure, all in neighborhoods that didn't necessarily sign up to host a driverless car depot.

The D.C. drivers' anxiety points to a bigger reckoning. Ride-share work has functioned for over a decade as a flexible income source for people who need one — a status quo that autonomous vehicles are built to disrupt. Unlike a human driver, a Waymo doesn't need a fare that covers rent; the economics tilt toward whoever owns the fleet. If Waymo's expansion into a major East Coast city succeeds, it will be the clearest test yet of whether robotaxis compress a driver's earning power, and by how much.

The Geely import strategy adds another layer. By sourcing vehicles from China rather than paying a premium for U.S.-tariffed EVs, Waymo is making a bet that speed and cost matter more than domestic sourcing optics — even as trade tensions over Chinese manufacturing remain politically charged in Washington. It's a reminder that a robotaxi company's supply chain is as consequential to its story as its software.

Taken together, these episodes describe a familiar pattern in how transformative technology actually arrives: not with a single triumphant unveiling, but through a hundred local skirmishes — a city council vote here, a nervous gig worker there, a tariff dodge tucked into a procurement decision. Waymo's core technology may be ready for prime time. Whether the cities, workers, and regulations around it are ready for Waymo is still, block by block, being decided.
