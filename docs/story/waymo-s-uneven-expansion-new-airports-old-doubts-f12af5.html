Waymo's Uneven Expansion: New Airports, Old Doubts

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-13

Waymo this month opened its robotaxi service to two more airports, adding Nashville International and Las Vegas's Harry Reid International — the fastest-growing airport in the country by passenger traffic — to a route map that already touches Phoenix, San Francisco and Los Angeles. The expansions, announced within days of each other, let riders summon a driverless Jaguar straight from arrivals rather than hailing a human-driven cab or rideshare. In Philadelphia, meanwhile, Waymo has logged more than a year of testing without naming a launch date, a silence the company has not explained and that locals have noticed.

The contrast is the story. Waymo, the Alphabet-owned company that has spent a decade and a reported tens of billions of dollars building the technical and regulatory case for driverless cars, is moving at two speeds at once: aggressive in markets that reward splashy headlines and tourist dollars, cautious in places where the terrain — literally and politically — seems trickier. Airports are good theater: steady demand, predictable routes, a captive audience of travelers happy to skip surge pricing. Philadelphia's dense grid, older infrastructure and unresolved state-level rules for autonomous vehicles make for a harder sell.

That caution looks more reasonable against the week's other headlines. In Tampa, University of Tampa students described a Waymo ride that went sideways, the kind of local news segment that rarely moves markets but chips away at public confidence one anecdote at a time. And in a public spat that says as much about the robotaxi business as any safety report, Elon Musk called Waymo's vehicles "pet destroyers," sharpening a rivalry between Tesla's camera-only robotaxi ambitions and Waymo's heavier, sensor-laden approach. Musk's insult was crude, but it landed in a moment when Waymo is trying to project unimpeachable safety as its core selling point against a competitor with fewer vehicles on the road but a louder megaphone.

None of this is fatal to Waymo's momentum — the company now operates in more cities than any rival, and airport access is a meaningful commercial foothold, since airports are where many Americans will have their first encounter with a driverless car. But the unevenness matters beyond optics. Every new airport launch invites regulators and the public to ask why Philadelphia, a top-ten US media market with its own airport and dense urban core, remains stuck in perpetual testing. Every viral bad-ride story and every Musk jab adds friction to an industry still trying to convince skeptical city councils, insurers and pedestrians that robotaxis are safer than the human drivers they're meant to replace. Waymo's expansion is real, but so is the gap between its glossiest announcements and its quietest hesitations — and that gap, more than any single ride gone wrong, is what will shape how fast driverless cars actually spread.
