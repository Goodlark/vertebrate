Waymo Presses Into Cincinnati and Denver as San Diego Pushes Back

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-12

Waymo's white-and-gray Jaguars have started appearing on Cincinnati streets this month, not yet carrying passengers but mapping the city's roads in the methodical, camera-and-lidar-laden way that precedes every launch. The company has not named a start date for public rides, only confirmed that the mapping phase — the unglamorous groundwork of teaching a car to recognize a particular pothole or a particular left turn onto Vine Street — is underway. It is the same choreography Waymo has run in more than a dozen cities now, and Cincinnati becomes the latest name on a list that keeps growing faster than most people outside the industry seem to notice.

In Denver, meanwhile, the reality is smaller than the headlines suggested when Waymo announced its arrival in Colorado. According to Denverite, the fleet actually operating there remains modest for now, a reminder that "Waymo has launched in your city" and "Waymo is a meaningful presence in your city" are two different claims. The company tends to enter a market quietly, with a handful of vehicles and a limited service area, long before it becomes the ambient hum of traffic that Phoenix or San Francisco residents now take for granted.

That slow-build approach is precisely what's being tested in San Diego, where the vehicles are no longer novel and some city councilmembers are uneasy about it. Councilmembers there are asking for outside help — from the state, presumably, since California cities have limited authority over autonomous vehicle permitting — to get a handle on Waymo's growing footprint. It's a notable admission: the people closest to the streets in question don't feel they have the tools to manage what's already driving on them.

Together, these three data points sketch the actual shape of the robotaxi rollout in America, which is less a single dramatic unveiling than a rolling, uneven expansion — mapping here, a small fleet there, friction somewhere else. For the robotics and AV industry, the pattern matters because it shows growth outpacing the regulatory scaffolding meant to contain it. Cities are discovering that "welcoming a pilot program" and "governing a fleet" are different jobs, and that the second one often arrives before anyone has agreed on the rules.

For residents, the stakes are less abstract: a new mode of transportation is arriving on their block whether or not their city council has settled on how to think about it. San Diego's request for help is a quiet signal that the honeymoon period — when a self-driving car was a curiosity worth a photo — is giving way to something more familiar in urban life: a fight over who gets to set the terms for shared streets.
