Waymo Hits Friction on Two Fronts: Minneapolis Bans Driverless Cars, Florida Roads Bite Back

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-01

Minneapolis this week became the latest city to draw a hard line on autonomous vehicles, narrowly passing an ordinance that requires a human driver behind the wheel of any robotaxi operating within city limits. The vote, close enough to suggest real disagreement on the city council rather than a rubber stamp, effectively ends Waymo's driverless ambitions there before the company ever got a real foothold. The ordinance doesn't ban Waymo by name, but the requirement for a human in the driver's seat defeats the entire premise of the service — there is no business case for a robotaxi that still needs a chauffeur.

The timing is awkward, because nearly thirteen hundred miles south, Waymo's cars are already on the road and discovering just how much a human driver's instincts are still worth. In South Florida, according to reporting from NBC 6, the company's vehicles have been struggling with the region's traffic culture — a place where lane markings function more as suggestions, turn signals are optional, and intersections operate on a kind of improvisational logic that no amount of lidar mapping fully anticipates. Add in the gaps in local infrastructure, from inconsistent signage to construction zones that change week to week, and you get a portrait of a technology built for order meeting a road system that runs on something closer to vibes.

Together, the two stories mark a shift in the conversation around Waymo, which for the past year has been defined almost entirely by expansion — new cities, new airport routes, new metro areas added to the map with the steady cadence of a company that believed its biggest obstacle was scale. What Minneapolis and Miami suggest instead is that Waymo's path forward isn't just an engineering problem to be solved with more data and more sensors; it's a negotiation with local politics and local driving culture, two things that don't update with a software patch.

The Minneapolis ordinance is particularly significant because it offers other city councils a template: rather than fighting a public battle over whether driverless cars are safe, officials can simply mandate a human presence and let the economics do the rest. It's a quieter, more durable form of resistance than outright prohibition, and other cities wary of ceding their streets to a California company's test fleet may well borrow the approach.

Meanwhile, the Florida friction points to a more fundamental limit on autonomy's promise. Waymo has built its reputation on cities with relatively legible traffic patterns — grid systems, enforced lane discipline, predictable pedestrian behavior. South Florida offers none of that, and the company's struggles there are a reminder that "self-driving" has always meant self-driving under specific, favorable conditions. As Waymo pushes into messier American cities, both the regulatory and the literal road ahead look considerably less smooth than the sunbelt suburbs where the technology first proved itself.
