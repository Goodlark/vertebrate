Waymo's Expansion Spree: 20,000 Cars, San Antonio, Central New York, and Madrid

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-20

Waymo is moving on four fronts simultaneously, and the arithmetic alone tells the story: the Alphabet-owned robotaxi company is preparing to grow its fleet from roughly 4,000 vehicles to 20,000, according to Reuters, a fivefold leap that would make it the largest driverless taxi operator in the country by a wide margin. The expansion isn't theoretical. Baby-blue Jaguar I-Paces outfitted with Waymo's sensor domes have already begun appearing on streets in San Antonio, and residents of Central New York have started spotting the same vehicles, enough to generate local speculation about what a Waymo presence might mean for the region's tech ambitions. Overseas, the company announced its first European market, pledging a fully autonomous ride-hailing launch in Madrid by 2027.

Taken individually, each of these is a modest data point — a new city here, a foreign announcement there. Taken together, they describe a company that has decided the cautious, one-metro-at-a-time approach of its early years is over. Waymo spent the better part of a decade testing in Phoenix and San Francisco before expanding gradually to Los Angeles and Austin. The jump to 20,000 vehicles, and the simultaneous push into a mid-sized Texas city, an upstate New York region, and a European capital, suggests a company racing to lock in geography before competitors — or regulators — catch up.

That matters beyond the novelty of a car with no one in the driver's seat. A fleet of 20,000 vehicles would put Waymo's operation in the same rough order of magnitude as a meaningful slice of traditional taxi and ride-hailing fleets in major American cities, which changes the conversation from "interesting pilot" to "infrastructure." It also puts fresh pressure on Uber and Lyft, both of which have been forced into uneasy partnerships with autonomous vehicle makers rather than compete head-on, and on cruise and delivery rivals still rebuilding after stumbles of their own.

The local dimension is just as telling. In San Antonio, the arrival of the cars is a visible, photographable event — people are already asking what the sensors on the roof do. In Central New York, where Waymo has offered no operational details, the mere appearance of test vehicles is enough to fuel talk of a tech-economy boomlet, a reminder of how hungry smaller metro areas are for a sign that they, too, might land a piece of the AI-adjacent economy, jobs and tax base included.

Madrid is the bigger signal. A 2027 launch date gives Waymo roughly two years to navigate European regulators, who have historically been warier of autonomous vehicles than their American counterparts, and to adapt its technology to narrower streets, different signage, and driving norms shaped by decades of urban planning Americans never had to contend with. Succeeding there would validate the company's core bet: that the hardest part of robotaxis was never a single city, but proving the model travels.
