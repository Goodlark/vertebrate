Tesla's Optimus Timeline Wobbles Between Hype and Delay

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-22

Tesla has begun what it calls commercial-scale production of its Optimus humanoid robot, with limited units reportedly rolling off a line in Fremont, California, even as Elon Musk told investors this week that the company plans to start selling the machines next year. But according to Electrek, which cites people familiar with the program, Tesla has quietly pushed full-volume deliveries to late 2027 — a two-year slip the company has not announced publicly. The sticking point, per the report, is unglamorous but fundamental: the robot's hands still can't reliably do the dexterous work — gripping, manipulating, handling objects with any nuance — that would make Optimus useful outside a stage demo.

Musk, for his part, has offered a different register of update. Asked about training progress, he compared the process to raising a toddler, a metaphor that is charming, faintly profound, and tells you almost nothing about torque curves or actuator yields. It's the kind of line that plays well in a keynote and poorly as an engineering status report, and it arrived in the same week that 24/7 Wall St. published an analysis treating "millions of units" of Optimus production as settled fact, with Texas Instruments flagged as a downstream beneficiary of all those future robot hands needing chips.

The gap between these two timelines — Musk's "next year," and the factory's "late 2027" — matters because Optimus has become a load-bearing pillar of Tesla's valuation story, one that lets a car company trade like an AI company. Investors who've priced in robot revenue, and suppliers positioning for a windfall, are reacting to the toddler metaphor and the "millions of units" framing rather than to the actual bottleneck, which is a decades-old problem in robotics: hands are hard. Dexterous manipulation has stumped labs at Boston Dynamics, Figure, and university robotics departments for years, and no amount of Fremont tooling makes gripping an egg, or a wrench, trivial.

This isn't academic for the broader humanoid-robot industry, which is racing Tesla on parallel timelines and watching closely to see whether Optimus becomes proof of concept or cautionary tale. A slip from "next year" to "2027" is a meaningful data point about how hard the last-mile problem of embodied AI actually is, even for a company with Tesla's manufacturing muscle and Musk's gift for narrative compression. For workers in warehouses and factories who've been told humanoid robots are imminently coming for repetitive tasks, and for policymakers drafting labor and safety rules around them, the real timeline is the one measured in hand actuators that don't yet work, not the one measured in Musk's next earnings call. The toddler, it turns out, is still learning to hold a spoon.
