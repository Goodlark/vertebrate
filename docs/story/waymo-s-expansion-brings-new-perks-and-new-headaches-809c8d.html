Waymo's Expansion Brings New Perks and New Headaches

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-12

In San Francisco this week, a Waymo passenger discovered a small, specific horror of the autonomous age: the car stopped, the doors wouldn't open, and there was no driver to ask for help. The ride ended not with a human hand on the latch but with a remote customer-support agent, somewhere offstage, clicking a button to spring the lock. The passenger got out. The story got around. It was a minor malfunction with an outsized moral — a reminder that in a car with no one driving, there is also, often, no one to ask.

The episode arrived amid a broader rollout. Waymo this week launched a transit rewards program in the Bay Area, offering riders a $2.85 credit for trips that pair a robotaxi ride with public transit — a nudge toward treating the driverless car not as a replacement for the bus or BART but as a feeder into it. It's a modest sum, calibrated less to move markets than to shape habits, and it signals something about where Waymo wants to sit in the civic imagination: not a novelty, not a threat to transit, but a cog in the machinery of getting around.

Both stories — the stuck door and the transit credit — are footnotes to a larger one that The New Yorker took up this week: the strange, incremental business of a city teaching itself to live alongside cars that have no one in the driver's seat. In San Francisco and Phoenix, where Waymo's white Jaguars and minivans have become as unremarkable as delivery trucks, residents describe an ambivalence that doesn't resolve so much as settle into routine — the mild thrill of the first ride curdling into the background irritation of a robotaxi idling at a crosswalk, or blocking a bike lane, or simply existing as an unblinking presence at 2 a.m.

That ambivalence is the real story of Waymo's expansion, and it matters beyond the two cities where it's unfolding. The company's bet is that autonomous vehicles will graduate from curiosity to infrastructure, the way the elevator or the escalator did — technologies nobody remarks on because they've simply absorbed them. But infrastructure earns public trust through reliability, not marketing gestures, and a locked door with a passenger inside is the kind of failure that lingers in a way no transit credit can offset. It touches something more primal than convenience: the sense of being in control of one's own exit.

For the robotics industry, the stakes are not abstract. Waymo is the furthest-along example of driverless technology meeting mass urban life, and its missteps become data points in a debate that regulators, insurers, and rival companies are all watching closely. Every awkward moment — a trapped rider, a double-parked robotaxi, a transit credit that reads as both perk and public-relations balm — becomes evidence in the argument over whether autonomous vehicles deserve the city streets they're increasingly occupying, or whether the city is simply the one doing the adjusting.
