Neuralink Opens Pivotal FDA Trial, Musk Floats Chronic Pain as Next Target

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-25

Neuralink said this week that it has received approval from the Food and Drug Administration to begin its first pivotal trial of its brain-computer interface, the regulatory threshold that stands between an experimental device and one a company can eventually bring to market. The announcement, posted by the company, opened recruitment to paralyzed patients, including those with ALS, the progressive neurodegenerative disease that steadily strips people of the ability to move and, eventually, to speak. It is the clearest sign yet that the company, co-founded by Elon Musk in 2016, is moving from demonstration to something resembling a medical product.

The timing of what followed was hard to miss. On the same day, Musk floated a considerably larger ambition for the implant: treating chronic neck and back pain, a condition that afflicts a vast swath of the adult population and has nothing to do with paralysis or motor-neuron disease. It was a Musk-ian move, pairing a hard regulatory milestone with a soft, sweeping promise, and it illustrated the gap that has defined Neuralink's public life since its first human implant — the distance between what the company is cleared to do and what its founder says it might someday do.

For the robotics and neurotechnology field, the pivotal trial matters more than any tweet. Earlier Neuralink studies were feasibility trials, small and exploratory, designed to show a device could be implanted safely and do something useful, like let a paralyzed person move a cursor by thought. A pivotal trial is different: it is the data-gathering exercise regulators use to decide whether to approve a device for real patients, at scale, under FDA oversight. Reaching this stage puts Neuralink in the company of a small number of implanted neural-interface efforts, including academic programs and rivals like Synchron, that are racing to turn decades of lab research into something insurers might someday cover.

The human stakes are concrete. For people with ALS, who lose motor control while often retaining full cognitive awareness, a functioning brain-computer interface could restore communication and a measure of independence long after speech and movement fail — a prospect that explains why ALS patients have been frequent, visible advocates for this technology. That is a narrow, well-defined population with urgent need and little to lose.

Chronic back and neck pain is a different universe of ambition, afflicting tens of millions of people for reasons ranging from disc degeneration to simple wear, and currently treated with physical therapy, medication, or surgery rather than implanted electrodes near the brain. Musk's suggestion, offered without data or a trial pathway attached, is the kind of forward-leaning claim that has drawn scrutiny before — it widens the aperture on what Neuralink might eventually promise shareholders and patients alike, even as the company's actual, regulator-sanctioned work remains focused on a much smaller and more desperate population waiting for the trial to begin enrolling them.
