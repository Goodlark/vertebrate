Waymo's Expansion Push: Tokyo Yes, Raleigh Maybe

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-30

Waymo and Toyota announced this week that they will begin testing autonomous bZ4X electric vehicles on Japanese public roads in 2027, the first joint deployment between the two companies and the clearest signal yet that Waymo intends to take its self-driving platform beyond American borders. The pilot will use Toyota's electric crossover, built in Japan, retrofitted with Waymo's sensor suite and software — a pairing that gives the American robotaxi company a foothold in a market long skeptical of ceding the wheel to software, and gives Toyota, which has watched more nimble rivals dominate the autonomy conversation, a credible answer to the question of what it's doing about self-driving cars.

The contrast with Waymo's domestic posture could hardly be sharper. In Raleigh this week, a company representative made the rounds with Triangle residents and offered almost nothing concrete — no launch date, no service boundaries, not even a firm commitment that robotaxis are coming at all. It was the rhetorical equivalent of a shrug delivered in a blazer: polite, noncommittal, designed to keep expectations low while keeping the door open.

That split screen — bold overseas commitments paired with hedged domestic ones — says something about where Waymo believes its leverage lies. In the U.S., the company already operates in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles and a handful of other cities, but each expansion has required painstaking negotiation with local regulators, transit officials and wary city councils, not to mention the occasional vandalized vehicle. Tokyo, by contrast, offers a tightly regulated, single national framework and a government eager to position Japan as an autonomous-vehicle testing ground, now that Toyota, its industrial crown jewel, is co-signing the project.

For the robotics and automotive industries, the Toyota deal matters beyond its symbolism. It links Waymo's software — widely regarded as the most road-tested autonomous stack in the world, after more than twenty million rider-only miles — with Toyota's manufacturing scale and its hard-won reputation for reliability. That combination could do more to normalize robotaxis internationally than any number of incremental U.S. city launches, especially in markets where consumers have been reluctant to trust Silicon Valley with their commutes.

But the Raleigh visit is a reminder that even the industry leader hasn't solved its oldest problem: scaling trust at home, one skeptical city council meeting at a time. Each new American market requires Waymo to relitigate questions of safety, liability and curb space that Tokyo's regulators may simply decide by fiat. For Triangle residents wondering when they'll see a Jaguar I-Pace glide driverless down Hillsborough Street, the honest answer, delivered this week, was: not yet, maybe not soon, and nobody's promising anything. Meanwhile, in Japan, the calendar already says 2027.
