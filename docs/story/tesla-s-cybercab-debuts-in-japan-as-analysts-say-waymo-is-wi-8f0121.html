Tesla's Cybercab Debuts in Japan as Analysts Say Waymo Is Winning the Robotaxi Race

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-11

Tesla put its Cybercab prototype on public display in Japan this week, the first time the steering-wheel-less, two-seat robotaxi has been shown outside the United States, according to Japan Wire, a Kyodo News affiliate. The unveiling gave Japanese media and consumers their first close look at the vehicle Elon Musk has billed as the future of Tesla, a low-slung pod with no pedals and no wheel, built for a driverless world Tesla has yet to prove it can actually operate in.

The timing was either unfortunate or perfectly on brand. The same day, Seeking Alpha published an analysis arguing that Tesla's stock has drifted well ahead of its self-driving reality, and that Waymo, the Alphabet-owned robotaxi operator, is quietly widening its lead in the one metric that matters most: cars actually driving themselves, with no one behind the wheel, carrying paying passengers. Waymo has been doing this for years now in Phoenix, San Francisco, and Los Angeles, expanding city by city with a fleet already logging millions of driverless miles. Tesla, by contrast, has spent a decade promising full autonomy is imminent.

The contrast matters because Tesla's valuation has never behaved like that of a car company. Investors have long paid a premium for the promise that Tesla will crack robotaxis at scale, turning idle vehicles into revenue-generating fleets and Musk's "self-driving" ambitions into an entirely new business line worth more than the cars themselves. A prototype tour through Tokyo is exactly the kind of spectacle that keeps that story alive: sleek, futuristic, good for headlines and social feeds, even if the vehicle isn't yet carrying a single paying rider anywhere in Japan, or most of the United States.

That gap between showmanship and deployment is the crux of the Seeking Alpha argument. Waymo isn't unveiling prototypes at auto shows; it's quietly signing up riders through an app, absorbing the unglamorous work of regulatory approval, mapping, and safety validation city by city. It's a slower, less cinematic path, but it's one measured in rides completed rather than press photos taken.

For the robotics and autonomous-vehicle industry, the divergence is instructive. Robotaxis were supposed to be the proving ground where software-defined transportation finally arrived, and for years Tesla set the terms of that conversation through sheer narrative force. Now the terms are shifting toward operational proof: who is actually driving passengers, in which cities, under what safety record. Cybercab's Tokyo debut was a marketing win, another data point in Tesla's global brand story. But brand stories don't get you a taxi medallion, or a regulator's sign-off, or a customer's trust at 2 a.m. on an empty street. Those things, so far, belong to Waymo.
