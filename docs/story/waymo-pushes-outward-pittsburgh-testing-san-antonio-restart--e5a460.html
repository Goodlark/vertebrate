Waymo Pushes Outward: Pittsburgh Testing, San Antonio Restart, and a 2028 Singapore Launch

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-18

Waymo announced this week that it will bring its robotaxi service to Singapore by 2028, working with the city-state's Ministry of Transport and Land Transport Authority, even as the company quietly resumed rides in San Antonio after a five-month suspension and began fully autonomous testing on the streets of Pittsburgh. Three cities, three continents' worth of ambition, one company betting that the slow grind of American regulatory patience and the clean-slate appeal of a tightly governed Asian city-state can both pay off at once.

The San Antonio restart is the least glamorous of the three announcements, and probably the most instructive. Waymo has not said in detail why it paused service there five months ago, but the return suggests the company treats its markets less like permanent footholds and more like experiments that can be switched off and rebooted as conditions warrant. That's a different posture than the one Waymo projects publicly, where every new city is framed as evidence of unstoppable momentum. The reality, at least in Texas, looks more like careful triage.

Pittsburgh is the newer story: fully autonomous testing, meaning no human safety driver behind the wheel, on a street grid that is notoriously unkind to any driver, human or otherwise, with its blind hills, narrow bridges and rivers that force traffic into chokepoints. If Waymo's vehicles can handle Pittsburgh's topography without a chaperone, that's a real technical marker, not just a press release milestone, and it matters for a company that has built its reputation on methodical expansion rather than Silicon Valley bravado.

Singapore is the headline-grabber, and for good reason. It would be Waymo's first launch outside the United States, and the partnership structure, working directly with a national transport ministry rather than city hall, signals a different kind of deal: one negotiated at the level of national infrastructure policy. Singapore is an attractive proving ground precisely because it is small, wealthy, and famously willing to engineer its streets and rules around new technology, from congestion pricing to car-ownership quotas. A government that already treats mobility as a policy lever to pull is a much easier partner than a patchwork of American city councils and state DMVs.

Taken together, these three moves describe a company trying to prove two things simultaneously: that it can operate durably in the messy, litigious, locally-governed American market, and that it has outgrown the need to prove that only at home. For the robotaxi industry more broadly, still mostly confined to a handful of Sunbelt cities and still dogged by safety scrutiny after incidents involving Waymo and its rivals, the Singapore deal offers a preview of what expansion might look like once the technology stops being treated as a local experiment and starts being negotiated as national infrastructure. That shift, more than any single city launch, is the real news here.
