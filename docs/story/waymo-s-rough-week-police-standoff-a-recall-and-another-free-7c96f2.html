Waymo's Rough Week: Police Standoff, a Recall, and Another Freeway Retreat

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-22

Over the span of a few days this month, Waymo's driverless cars managed to get turned around by Phoenix police at an active investigation scene, triggered a recall over the vehicles' trouble following direct human instructions, and were pulled off freeways in several markets for the second time this year. Each incident is small on its own. Together they read like a company hitting the limits of a technology that has, until recently, seemed almost suspiciously smooth.

The Phoenix episode was the most mundane and the most telling. A Waymo vehicle, navigating near an active police investigation, had to be manually redirected by officers on scene — the kind of real-time judgment call that humans make by reading body language, hand signals, and the general chaos of an emergency, and that a robot, however well-trained, still finds opaque. It is the latest entry in a running tension between autonomous vehicles and the police departments that have to manage them in the field, a relationship that remains more improvised than anyone at Waymo would like to admit.

The recall, meanwhile, cuts closer to the core promise of the technology. Waymo said it found its vehicles were not reliably responding to human override instructions — the fail-safe that is supposed to let a person step in when the software gets confused. A self-driving car that struggles to take direction from a human is not a minor bug; it is a crack in the premise that these vehicles can always be stopped, redirected, or corrected when something goes wrong. The company has not detailed how many vehicles are affected or what the fix involves, but the timing, layered onto the Phoenix incident, suggests a pattern rather than a coincidence.

Then there is the freeway retreat. Waymo has again withdrawn its robotaxis from freeway driving, a maneuver it has made before and reversed before, suggesting that high-speed, high-consequence driving remains a harder problem than city streets full of pedestrians and stoplights. The pullback arrives just as Tesla is closing the registration gap in Texas, pushing more of its own vehicles onto the road and chipping away at what had been a comfortable Waymo lead in deployed autonomous miles.

None of this amounts to a crisis for Waymo, which still operates the most extensively tested robotaxi fleet in the country and carries paying passengers daily in multiple cities. But the stumbles matter because they puncture the narrative of inevitability that has surrounded the company — the sense that autonomous driving was simply a matter of scaling up a solved problem. Recalls and freeway retreats are the unglamorous mechanics of an industry still working out its edge cases, and edge cases are precisely where public trust gets made or lost. For an industry whose economics depend on convincing regulators, insurers, and ordinary riders that machines can be trusted with judgment calls humans make instinctively, a rough week like this one is a reminder that the hardest ten percent of the problem is still, stubbornly, the whole point.
