Waymo Pushes Its Robotaxi Map Outward, From Denver to Evanston

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-19

Waymo is once again redrawing its map. The Alphabet-owned robotaxi company says it expects to open a public service in Denver later this year, according to CBS News, while in Evanston, Illinois, residents have begun spotting the company's white Jaguar SUVs, sensor pods spinning, gliding down quiet residential streets — not yet picking up passengers, but doing the preparatory work of mapping that precedes any launch, as the Evanston RoundTable first reported.

The two moves look modest on their own — a promised launch date in one city, a handful of camera-topped cars in another — but together they read as the clearest signal yet that Waymo has settled into a steady rhythm of expansion, and has no intention of stopping in the two markets, San Francisco and Phoenix, where it built its reputation and its early ridership.

That rhythm matters because Waymo remains the only company operating a genuine driverless taxi service at scale in the United States, after Cruise's retreat from robotaxis and the slower, more cautious rollouts pursued by rivals like Zoox. Every new city is both a test of the technology's ability to generalize — snow in Denver, narrow tree-lined streets and four-way stops in Evanston — and a test of whether local governments, insurers and residents will accept driverless cars as an ordinary part of the streetscape rather than a novelty or a hazard.

The mapping work spotted in Evanston is, by design, unglamorous. Before Waymo ever opens a service to riders, it sends vehicles — often with a human safety driver — to drive every block repeatedly, building the high-resolution maps its software relies on to distinguish a mailbox from a pedestrian, a bike lane from a turn lane. It is the least visible part of the company's operation and also the most essential, the equivalent of a surveyor walking a property line before anyone breaks ground. That it is happening in a Chicago suburb, rather than a Sunbelt boomtown, suggests Waymo is also testing its technology against denser, older urban grids of the kind common across the Midwest and Northeast.

For the robotics and autonomous-vehicle industry, each new city is a data point in an argument the sector has been trying to win since Waymo spun out of Google's self-driving project more than a decade ago: that autonomy can move beyond a handful of sunny, wide-laned test markets into the messier average of American streets. For residents of Denver and Evanston, the arrival of these cars — first as quiet surveyors, later as paying rides — will mean adjusting to a new kind of neighbor on the road, one that doesn't get tired, doesn't get distracted, and doesn't, yet, entirely explain itself.
