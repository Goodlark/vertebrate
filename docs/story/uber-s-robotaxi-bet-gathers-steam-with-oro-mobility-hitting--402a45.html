Uber's Robotaxi Bet Gathers Steam, With Oro Mobility Hitting 6 Million Miles

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-08

Oro Mobility, the Hertz-backed autonomous vehicle company building toward a robotaxi partnership with Uber, said this week that its test fleet has now logged more than 6 million miles, a milestone the company is treating as proof it is closing in on a commercial launch. The figure was disclosed as part of an update on the venture, which pairs Hertz's rental-car scale and fleet-management muscle with Uber's ride-hailing network — the kind of alliance that increasingly defines how legacy transportation companies are trying to get into the self-driving business without building an AV stack from scratch.

The mileage milestone landed the same week that Gary Black, the veteran investor and managing partner at Future Fund, made a public case that Uber, not Tesla or Waymo, is best positioned to bring robotaxis to a true mass market. Black's argument, aired on Yahoo Finance, is less about any single technological breakthrough than about distribution: Uber already has tens of millions of riders trained to open an app and hail a car, a demand-side advantage that neither Tesla's manufacturing-first approach nor Waymo's slower, city-by-city rollout can easily replicate.

Taken together, the Oro news and Black's thesis describe the same bet from two angles — one operational, one financial — and both point toward the same conclusion: the robotaxi race may be decided less by who has the best sensors or the cleverest neural net, and more by who already owns the customer relationship. Uber, for all the years it spent insisting it was not in the business of owning cars, has quietly repositioned itself as the marketplace layer that autonomous vehicle operators need to reach riders at scale. That is precisely the role Oro is being built to fill on the supply side, with Hertz's fleet infrastructure — depots, maintenance, insurance relationships — doing the unglamorous work that self-driving hype tends to skip over.

For the robotaxi industry generally, the significance is less about six million miles being some magic threshold and more about what the accumulation signals: a second tier of AV programs, built on partnerships rather than proprietary end-to-end systems, is maturing alongside Waymo's more vertically integrated approach and Tesla's promised but still largely unrealized robotaxi ambitions. If Oro's miles translate into an actual commercial rollout on Uber's app, it would give riders in whatever cities are chosen a very ordinary-looking way to encounter an extraordinary technology — summon a car, watch it arrive with no one behind the wheel, and pay through an app they already have installed. That mundane user experience, more than any milestone number, is the real product being tested. And for investors like Black, the wager is that whoever controls that moment of ordinary convenience, not whoever owns the most sophisticated sensor suite, ends up owning the market.
