Neuralink's Halo Draws Scrutiny — and Rivals

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-01

Neuralink has not implanted a new patient, published a fresh peer-reviewed result, or announced a device upgrade in months. What it has done is get more expensive. The company's valuation has climbed sharply over the past year, according to Business Insider's reporting, in a run-up that owes more to Elon Musk's gravitational pull and investor appetite for anything adjacent to him than to any concrete clinical or technical milestone the company has disclosed. That gap — between the hype and the data — is starting to look less like a quirk and more like a vulnerability, just as the company's competitive position gets more crowded.

In China, regulators are moving to draft national standards for brain-computer interfaces, according to the South China Morning Post, a bureaucratic step that reads as a starting gun. Domestic firms are lining up to build implants that compete directly with Neuralink's, and a national standard is the kind of thing that tends to accelerate a market rather than slow it down — it tells manufacturers, hospitals, and investors that the state has decided this is a category worth organizing around. For a field that has largely been defined by one Silicon Valley company's press releases, the emergence of a state-backed Chinese effort changes the shape of the conversation considerably.

Then there is the stranger challenge, the one that doesn't require a neurosurgeon at all. A startup profiled by Fast Company is wagering that the tongue — dense with nerve endings, easy to access, in no need of a craniotomy — could serve as a viable interface between brain and machine, sidestepping the entire premise that you need a chip under the skull to read intent. It's a smaller, weirder bet than Neuralink's, but it points at the same insight the Chinese standards effort does: the "brain-computer interface" category is bigger than one company's surgical implant, and plenty of engineers think the hard problem can be solved without opening anyone's head.

None of this means Neuralink is in trouble, exactly. It remains the name most people reach for when they try to describe what a brain-computer interface even is, and that kind of brand recognition is worth something no standard or startup can easily replicate. But markets built on one company's mystique are fragile in a specific way: they need periodic proof. Neuralink's rivals are betting that if the proof doesn't come — another implant, another study, another public demonstration — the frenzy around its valuation will look, in hindsight, less like conviction and more like faith. For patients waiting on the promise of restored movement or speech, and for an industry trying to figure out which approach actually scales, that's not an academic distinction. It's the difference between a technology and a story.
