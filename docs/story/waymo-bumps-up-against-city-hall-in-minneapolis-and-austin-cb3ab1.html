Waymo Bumps Up Against City Hall in Minneapolis and Austin

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-16

Waymo's robotaxis have spent the past year gliding through American downtowns with the eerie competence of a chauffeur who never needs a coffee break. But this month, two cities are reminding the company that gliding through a city and governing one are different things. In Minneapolis, officials have told Waymo that its cars will not operate without a human sitting behind the wheel — a direct challenge to the driverless premise the company has spent a decade and billions of dollars building. And in Austin, where Waymo has been running fully driverless since last year, the city's parking enforcement division has quietly written the company 123 tickets since August 2024, according to records reviewed by KXAN.

The Minneapolis requirement is the more pointed of the two rebukes. Waymo's entire commercial pitch rests on the absence of a driver — that's the product, and the cost savings, and the sci-fi thrill that gets people to open the app. A safety monitor in the driver's seat turns a robotaxi back into something closer to a very expensive, very cautious rental car with a chaperone. City officials haven't laid out a public timeline for lifting the restriction, but the message is clear enough: before Minneapolis lets go of the wheel, someone accountable and human needs to be able to grab it back.

Austin's problem is smaller in scale but tells a stranger story. A parking ticket is the most mundane instrument of municipal power imaginable — a paper notice tucked under a wiper blade, backed by the assumption that a human owner will eventually pay it or fight it in traffic court. Waymo's vehicles have piled up 123 of them since last summer, and it's not clear the existing enforcement machinery was built with a fleet-operated, centrally dispatched car in mind at all. Who shows up to contest a violation? Does the fine go against a driver's record that doesn't exist? Austin's answer, so far, appears to be: figure it out as the tickets accumulate.

Together, the two episodes puncture a narrative Waymo has cultivated carefully — that autonomous driving is now a solved engineering problem simply awaiting rollout. The harder problem, it turns out, is bureaucratic: zoning-era rules written for human drivers don't bend easily around a fleet of software-piloted cars, and city halls, unlike venture capitalists, aren't in a hurry to be impressed. For the broader industry, these frictions matter more than any single crash report. Cruise's suspension in San Francisco showed that regulatory goodwill can evaporate overnight; Minneapolis and Austin show the more mundane version of that risk — not a catastrophic failure, but the slow grind of a hundred small municipal decisions, each one capable of blunting the economics of the driverless promise long before the technology itself ever falters.
