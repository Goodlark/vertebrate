Figure's Molten-Steel Demo Fuels Brett Adcock's Humanoid Hype Machine

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-05

This week Figure, the Bay Area humanoid-robotics startup, released footage of its machines working in close proximity to molten steel, a demonstration the company says points toward a future in which its robots handle industrial tasks too dangerous or unpleasant for human workers. The clip, reported first by Industrial Equipment News, shows the humanoid moving with apparent composure near the glowing vats that have, for the better part of a century, been the province of workers in heat-resistant suits and respirators. Figure did not specify a factory, a partner steelmaker, or a timeline for deployment. What it offered instead was an image: robot, fire, proof of concept.

That image arrives in the middle of a broader story, one Forbes laid out in a profile of Figure's founder, Brett Adcock, who has become something of a connoisseur of the industrial sublime. Adcock, the piece argues, has built Figure's public identity less through quiet pilot programs than through a steady cadence of dramatic footage and press attention, a strategy aimed as much at investors as at factory managers. The molten-steel video fits neatly into that pattern: spectacular, easy to share, and conspicuously short on the operational detail — duty cycles, safety certifications, cost per unit — that would tell a steel executive whether to actually buy the thing.

None of this makes the demo meaningless, exactly. Humanoid robotics is still a field trying to convince the world it deserves the money being poured into it, and in an industry starved for believable use cases, a robot standing near 2,800-degree metal is at least a vivid pitch. But the gap between demonstration and deployment is where the humanoid-robot boom has repeatedly stumbled. Robots that can walk across a stage are not the same as robots that can survive a full shift, pass an insurance audit, and get fixed by a plant technician who has never heard of Figure.

The stakes of that gap are not trivial. Humanoid robotics has become one of the most heavily capitalized corners of the robotics economy, with venture money betting that general-purpose machines will eventually do the unglamorous, hazardous work that keeps workers' comp lawyers employed — steel pouring, foundry labor, chemical handling. If the bet pays off, it could meaningfully reduce injuries in some of the most dangerous jobs in manufacturing. If it's mostly theater, it risks another cycle of inflated expectations crashing into the slow, unglamorous reality of industrial engineering, the kind that doesn't make for good footage.

For now, Figure's molten-steel clip functions less as evidence of a finished product than as a chapter in Adcock's larger narrative project — one in which the line between demonstration and advertisement is doing a lot of quiet work. The robots may well be capable of what the video suggests. But in an industry that has learned to be skeptical of highlight reels, the harder, less photogenic test is still ahead: whether the machine can do the job on a Tuesday, unsupervised, for a thousand hours in a row.
