Waymo's Driverless Cars Hit Denver Streets, Confusion Included

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-07

Waymo's driverless cars began operating on Denver streets this week with no safety driver behind the wheel, extending the company's robotaxi service beyond its established territories in Phoenix and San Francisco into a city with a decidedly messier relationship with pavement. The rollout came with an immediate, almost comic complication: a Waymo vehicle got stuck navigating active construction on Colfax Avenue, one of Denver's oldest and most notoriously chaotic corridors, unable to parse the cones, lane shifts, and improvised detours that any Denver driver learns to shrug off.

Local officials, meanwhile, are still figuring out the basics of how you police a car that has no driver to pull over. Traffic stops, parking citations, questions of liability after a fender-bender — the entire apparatus of American traffic enforcement assumes a human being sitting where the steering wheel is. Denver's police department and city agencies are now working through those questions in something close to real time, a scramble that other cities have faced as Waymo has expanded, but one that carries its own local texture given Denver's particular infrastructure headaches.

That the company's first real stumble here involves road construction is telling. Phoenix's grid is wide, sun-baked, and predictable; San Francisco's is dense but thoroughly mapped after years of testing. Denver's streets, by contrast, are in a near-constant state of repair, with construction zones that shift week to week and signage that even human drivers find maddening. For Waymo, that unpredictability is precisely the kind of edge case that autonomous systems have historically struggled with — not the dramatic, movie-ready failure, but the mundane one, a car simply not knowing what to do when the world doesn't match its map.

The stakes here extend well beyond one confused vehicle idling near a construction cone. Waymo has spent years arguing that its technology is ready to scale into more varied American cities, and Denver represents a test of that claim in terrain notably less forgiving than the company's original strongholds. If robotaxis can't reliably handle a city's ordinary chaos — the potholes, the impatient work crews, the cones that appear overnight — the promise of driverless expansion nationwide gets harder to sell to city officials and skeptical residents alike.

There's also a quieter, more consequential story underneath the construction-zone hiccup: the slow, improvisational rewriting of traffic law and enforcement to accommodate machines. Denver's scramble to figure out how you cite a car with nobody in it is a preview of a conversation cities across the country will eventually have to have, whether they're ready for it or not. Waymo's cars may eventually learn Colfax Avenue. The rest of us are still learning what rules apply once they do.
