Unitree Unveils 'Superman' Humanoid Robot, Touts Record-Breaking Speed

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-17

Unitree Robotics, the Hangzhou-based firm that has become China's most visible face of humanoid robotics, unveiled a new bipedal machine it calls "Superman" ahead of a debut in Shanghai, claiming the robot can run faster than Usain Bolt's peak sprint speed. The company did not release independent verification of the claim, but the announcement lands squarely in a season of one-upmanship among Chinese robot makers eager to prove that machines built to walk like us can also outrun us.

Bolt's fastest recorded speed, clocked during his 2009 world-record 100-meter dash in Berlin, hit roughly 27.8 miles per hour at his peak stride. Unitree's assertion that its new robot exceeds that figure is, for now, a marketing claim rather than a peer-reviewed benchmark. Still, the company has a track record of backing up its bravado: its H1 and G1 models have already drawn attention for running, jumping and doing backflips in demonstration videos that circulate widely on social media, part of a deliberate strategy of turning humanoid robots into viral spectacle.

The timing is not incidental. Shanghai has become something of a proving ground for China's humanoid robot ambitions, hosting robot competitions, industry expos and government-backed showcases meant to signal that the country intends to lead in what officials call "physical AI" — the fusion of large language models and machine learning with robots that can act in the physical world. Beijing has poured subsidies into humanoid robotics as a strategic industry, and firms like Unitree, along with rivals such as UBTech and Fourier Intelligence, are racing to demonstrate not just dexterity but raw athletic capability as proof of engineering maturity.

For the robotics industry, speed records make for splashy headlines, but they also serve a more practical purpose: locomotion benchmarks are a proxy for the quality of a robot's balance, actuator power and control software, all of which matter far beyond sprinting. A humanoid that can run stably at high speed is also one better equipped to navigate warehouses, disaster zones or factory floors without toppling over — the kinds of unglamorous tasks investors actually care about.

Yet the gap between demonstration and deployment remains wide. Sprinting robots make for great trade-show footage, but the commercial case for humanoids still hinges on cost, reliability and whether they can perform repetitive labor cheaper than a human worker or a purpose-built machine. Unitree's flashy unveiling is best understood as part of that longer campaign — to convince investors, governments and the public that the humanoid robot, long a science-fiction fixture, is now an industrial product worth betting on. Whether "Superman" can do anything more useful than run fast is the question that will determine if this moment is remembered as a turning point or merely a very fast photo op.
