Waymo's Growing Pains: Restrictions, Safety Complaints, and Labor Backlash

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-06

San Francisco spent last week's Fleet Week doing something it rarely does to Waymo: telling it no. City officials restricted the company's driverless taxis from parts of the waterfront during the air show and naval celebration, a crowd-control measure that also amounted to a small, symbolic rebuke of a service that has otherwise operated in the city with few formal limits. Three thousand miles away in Orlando, the enthusiast site Inside the Magic published an opinion piece accusing Waymo vehicles of flouting traffic laws near Universal's theme park gates, warning tourists to watch their step around the robotaxis. And in Washington, D.C., a union leader told FOX 5 that Waymo's expansion into the capital threatens thousands of local driving jobs.

None of these is, on its own, a crisis for Waymo. A parade-route detour, a blog post, a labor leader's warning — these are the ordinary friction of a company scaling fast. But taken together, they trace a pattern that has followed autonomous vehicles since the first test cars rolled through Phoenix: the technology expands faster than the civic machinery built to absorb it.

That gap matters because Waymo is no longer a novelty confined to a few sunbelt suburbs. It is now a fixture in San Francisco, Phoenix, Los Angeles, Austin, and is pushing into new cities like Orlando and Washington, each with its own traffic patterns, tourist density, and labor politics. A fender-bender near a theme park reads differently than one on a quiet residential street; a union leader's warning lands differently in a city where driving jobs are a meaningful rung on the economic ladder. Cities are discovering, case by case, that they have limited tools to shape how these services behave — Fleet Week's restriction being a rare example of a municipality asserting control, if only for a weekend.

The labor question is the more durable one. Taxi, rideshare, and delivery driving has long served as accessible work, especially in cities like Washington, where union leaders now see an employer-less employer arriving with no negotiating table at all. Waymo doesn't bargain with a union; it doesn't have a workforce to organize. That asymmetry — a tech platform replacing jobs without the usual channels for workers to push back — is likely to recur in every city the company enters, long after any single controversy about a near-miss by a theme park fades.

For the robotaxi industry more broadly, these frictions are a preview of the regulatory and political terrain ahead. Waymo has largely won the technical argument, racking up millions of driverless miles with a safety record it touts in every market entry. What it hasn't yet won is the harder argument — the one happening in city council chambers, union halls, and local news comment sections — about who gets to set the terms when a company's cars become part of a city's daily traffic, and part of its labor market, before anyone quite agreed to the arrangement.
