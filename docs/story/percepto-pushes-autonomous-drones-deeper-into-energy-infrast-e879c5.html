Percepto Pushes Autonomous Drones Deeper Into Energy Infrastructure Inspection

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-05

Percepto, the Israeli-American drone-automation company, announced two developments this week that together tighten its grip on the unglamorous but increasingly lucrative business of watching energy infrastructure so humans don't have to. First, it launched a next-generation inspection platform that pairs autonomous drone flight with AI-driven asset analysis, pitched squarely at utilities that want to inspect more substations, transformers, and transmission corridors without adding headcount. Second, and more consequentially, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency approved Percepto's autonomous optical gas imaging drones as an alternative test method under Subpart OOOOa and OOOOb — the federal rules governing methane leak detection at oil-and-gas facilities. That approval means operators can now send a drone instead of a technician to satisfy compliance checks that have, until now, required a person walking the site with a handheld camera.

The timing is not incidental. Both moves land as energy companies face a familiar squeeze: aging infrastructure that needs more frequent inspection, a labor market that has made it harder to keep skilled field crews fully staffed, and regulators who are no longer content with occasional, manual spot-checks. Percepto's answer to all three is the same drone sitting in a box on-site, launching itself on a schedule, flying a programmed route, and handing the resulting imagery to software that flags anomalies — a hot bolt, a leaking valve, a plume of methane invisible to the naked eye but not to an infrared sensor.

The EPA approval is the sharper of the two headlines, because it changes the economics of compliance rather than just the convenience of maintenance. Methane monitoring under OOOOa and OOOOb has traditionally meant dispatching workers to remote well pads, often in difficult terrain, to perform optical gas imaging surveys by hand. Letting a drone do that job as an officially sanctioned "alternative test method" removes both a safety exposure and a recurring labor cost from operators' books, at a moment when methane rules are tightening and enforcement scrutiny is rising. For an industry that has spent the last few years lobbying over the cost of emissions compliance, a federally blessed automated option is not a minor convenience — it is a lever on operating expense.

For the robotics industry more broadly, the news is a data point in a familiar pattern: autonomy earns its keep not through spectacle but through regulatory plumbing. Warehouse robots needed insurers to sign off; self-driving cars needed state DMVs. Now inspection drones need the EPA, and Percepto appears to have gotten there first in this particular corner of energy infrastructure. That kind of approval is sticky — competitors will need their own sign-off to match it, and utilities and oil-and-gas operators tend to be conservative buyers who favor whichever vendor already cleared the regulatory bar. If methane and asset inspection become default drone jobs rather than novelties, the quieter effect will be felt by the field technicians and inspection contractors whose work increasingly consists of reviewing a dashboard rather than driving to a well pad.
