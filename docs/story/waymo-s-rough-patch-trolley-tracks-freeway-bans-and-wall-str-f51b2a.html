Waymo's Rough Patch: Trolley Tracks, Freeway Bans, and Wall Street Jitters

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-18

On a stretch of San Diego trolley track this month, a Waymo robotaxi did something no human driver would do twice: it pulled onto active rail, then backed up straight through a lowered crossing gate, snapping the arm as it retreated. No one was hurt, and no trolley was bearing down at the moment, but the video circulated quickly, another entry in the growing file of things Waymo's software apparently did not expect to encounter. Days later, California regulators delivered a blunter verdict, declaring the company's driverless vehicles an "imminent hazard" and ordering them off the state's freeways, even as Waymo continued to cite its own data showing its cars crash less often than human drivers do.

The two episodes landed in the same news cycle as a Barron's analysis arguing that Waymo's stumble is not just a Waymo problem. The company has long been the industry's proof of concept, the robotaxi operator furthest along in miles driven and cities served, the one Wall Street points to when it wants to argue autonomy is finally arriving. If Waymo can be caught on camera reversing through a railroad gate, and if California's own regulators are willing to override a market leader's safety statistics, then the entire investment thesis around self-driving cars looks shakier than the press releases suggest. Barron's drew a direct line to Tesla, whose stock price carries a hefty premium for a robotaxi future that Elon Musk has promised for years and has yet to deliver at any real scale. Waymo's trouble, in this reading, is a preview of the skepticism Tesla's own ambitions will eventually face once its cars are judged by incidents rather than keynote demos.

What makes the California order notable is that it didn't quibble with Waymo's statistics so much as sidestep them entirely. The company has built its public case on aggregate crash-rate comparisons, the kind of numbers that look reassuring in a chart but say little about the specific, embarrassing failure modes that show up on local news: a car confused by a trolley crossing, a system that regulators worried couldn't reliably handle freeway speeds and merges. That's the uncomfortable gap at the center of the robotaxi story right now. A fleet can be statistically safer than the average human driver and still do things that no licensed driver would do, because the failure isn't about caution or recklessness but about the software's blind spots in situations its training data didn't anticipate.

For the industry, the stakes go beyond one company's stock price. Robotaxis are being sold to cities, regulators, and investors as an inevitability, a technology whose rollout is a matter of when rather than if. Every geofenced neighborhood Waymo operates in, every freeway corridor it's allowed onto, represents a regulatory trust that took years to build. A reversed gate arm and a freeway ban don't erase that progress, but they are reminders that the public and the regulators granting these companies room to operate are still watching closely, and that trust, once shaken, is far harder to rebuild than a crash-rate spreadsheet can repair.
