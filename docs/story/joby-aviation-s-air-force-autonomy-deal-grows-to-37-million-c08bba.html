Joby Aviation's Air Force Autonomy Deal Grows to $37 Million

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-04

Joby Aviation said this week that it has expanded its autonomous flight contract with the U.S. Air Force to nearly $37 million, a milestone the Santa Cruz, California-based company tied directly to the success of its first coast-to-coast autonomous flight — a Cessna Caravan that flew 6,665 miles across the country without a pilot at the controls. The contract growth was disclosed alongside Joby's third-quarter 2026 earnings, in which the company raised its full-year revenue guidance by 15%, citing the completion of first test flights in Florida and Texas under a White House-backed air-taxi program.

The timing was no accident. Joby has spent years building toward a future in which its electric aircraft carry passengers over cities, but the Air Force money reflects a quieter, parallel bet: that the fastest path to revenue — and to proving out autonomy software in the real world — runs through defense contracts and cargo planes, not just the air-taxi routes that have dominated the company's public image. Flying a Cessna Caravan autonomously across the entire continental United States is the kind of unglamorous, grinding demonstration that the Pentagon tends to reward, and the Air Force's willingness to nearly double down on the contract suggests the flight did what it needed to do.

For Joby, a company that has long traded on the promise of ferrying commuters above Los Angeles freeways, the quarter is a reminder that the economics of advanced air mobility are still being worked out one government check at a time. Air-taxi certification remains a multi-year slog, and test flights in Florida and Texas under the administration's air-taxi initiative are useful mostly as proof that the aircraft can operate in ordinary American airspace without incident. The Air Force deal, by contrast, is cash in hand, tied to a capability — autonomous flight over long distances — that has obvious appeal well beyond the air-taxi market: logistics, surveillance, disaster response, and the slow military push toward uncrewed aerial logistics that has accelerated since the war in Ukraine made attritable drones a budget line item rather than a novelty.

The broader signal for the robotics and autonomy industry is that revenue is starting to follow the unglamorous work. Investors have spent years pricing eVTOL companies on the assumption that passenger service is the prize; what's emerging instead is a hybrid business model in which military autonomy contracts and air-taxi testing fund each other, with the Pentagon acting as an early, patient customer willing to pay for reliability long before paying commuters are allowed to climb aboard. A 15% bump in full-year guidance is not, by itself, proof that Joby has solved the harder problem of scaling passenger flights. But it does suggest that the company has found, at least for now, a paying audience for the thing it has actually built: a machine that can fly itself a very long way without anyone touching the stick.
