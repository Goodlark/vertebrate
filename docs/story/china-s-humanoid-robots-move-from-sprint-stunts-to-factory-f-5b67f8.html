China's Humanoid Robots Move From Sprint Stunts to Factory Floors and Export Docks

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-10

The robot that beat Usain Bolt is getting a new job. Tiangong Ultra, the humanoid built by the Beijing Innovation Center for Human Robotics, made headlines this spring when it out-sprinted the world's fastest man in a widely circulated demonstration. Now, according to reporting from the Japan Times, its handlers are retooling it for something less telegenic: sorting parts, moving materials, standing in for humans on assembly lines. The pivot is not really about one robot. It is about an industry deciding it has proven its point and needs to start making money.

That shift shows up starkly in the export ledgers. New data cited by Gasgoo puts China's share of global humanoid robot exports at 97%, a figure so dominant it borders on monopoly. Just five Chinese manufacturers account for nearly three-quarters of all shipments worldwide, evidence that the industry, while still young, is consolidating fast around a handful of well-funded players with the manufacturing muscle to move from prototypes to production lines.

This matters because humanoid robotics has spent the last few years as a spectacle business — a parade of backflips, sprints, and dance routines designed to win headlines and venture capital rather than payrolls. China's approach has always been more mercantile: build the supply chain, own the components, then flood the market. The country already dominates the actuators, gearboxes, and battery systems that go into these machines, so the leap from component supplier to finished-goods exporter was less a moonshot than a natural extension of an existing industrial base.

For the global robotics industry, the numbers are a warning shot. Companies in the U.S., Japan, and Korea that have spent years perfecting bipedal locomotion and dexterous hands now face a competitor that can undercut them on price at scale, the same playbook that reshaped solar panels, electric vehicles, and drones. A five-firm oligopoly controlling three-quarters of exports also means the terms of the emerging humanoid economy — pricing, safety standards, even the software stack — could be set largely in China before other countries have shipped their first commercial units.

For workers and factory owners, the more immediate story is Tiangong Ultra's career change. A robot famous for a novelty stunt is now a proof-of-concept for something with real economic weight: machines that can walk into existing warehouses and plants, built by legs rather than wheels, and take on tasks too repetitive or dangerous for people. If China can do for humanoids what it did for electric vehicles — turn a curiosity into a cheap, exportable commodity — the effect will be felt not in viral videos but in hiring decisions on factory floors from Ohio to Osaka.
