Tesla's Robotaxi Rolls Into San Antonio as China Sales Sputter

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-09

Tesla's Robotaxi service rolled into San Antonio this week, the latest dot on a map the company hopes will soon cover much of the country. The expansion, reported by Teslarati, gives Tesla a foothold in Texas's seventh-largest city and adds to a growing but still modest list of markets where riders can summon one of the company's autonomous vehicles. The timing was notable: the same day, Tesla disclosed that its overall China sales rose ten percent, even as sales inside China itself sank to a four-year low, according to Investor's Business Daily.

That split screen — a new American city for the robotaxi fleet, a shrinking home-turf market in China — captures the odd shape of Tesla's moment. The company is simultaneously trying to convince investors it has a software-and-services future, built on autonomy, while its core business of selling cars in the world's largest auto market keeps losing ground to cheaper, increasingly sophisticated domestic rivals like BYD.

San Antonio joins a short roster of cities where Tesla has switched on Robotaxi, a list the company has been building methodically rather than rushing nationwide. Each new city is a test not just of the technology but of the regulatory and reputational tolerance for autonomous vehicles sharing streets with pedestrians, cyclists, and skeptical city councils. Unlike Waymo, which has spent years accumulating permits and safety data city by city, Tesla has leaned on the promise of its camera-based "full self-driving" stack scaling faster and cheaper, without the lidar sensors and mapping infrastructure its rivals use. Whether that bet pays off in San Antonio, a sprawling, car-dependent city very different from the dense urban cores where ride-hailing usually thrives, will say something about how far the strategy generalizes.

The China numbers, meanwhile, are a reminder that Tesla's growth increasingly depends on exports and overseas assembly rather than demand from Chinese buyers themselves. A four-year low in domestic sales is not a rounding error; it reflects intensifying price wars and a flood of competitive electric models from local manufacturers who no longer need Tesla as a reference point for what a good EV looks like. The ten-percent rise in overall China sales, likely buoyed by vehicles built for other markets, papers over a weakening at home.

For the robotics and autonomy industry, the juxtaposition matters because it underscores how much of Tesla's valuation now rests on a narrative of autonomous services rather than vehicle sales growth. Investors have been asked to look past slowing car demand toward a future where Robotaxi fleets generate recurring revenue in city after city. Each incremental launch, San Antonio included, becomes a data point in that argument. But the China figures are a reminder that the old business still has to hold steady — or at least not collapse — while the new one tries to prove itself, one city at a time.
