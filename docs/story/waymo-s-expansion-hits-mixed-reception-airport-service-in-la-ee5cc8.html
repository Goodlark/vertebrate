Waymo's Expansion Hits Mixed Reception: Airport Service in Las Vegas, Eggs in Denver

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-21

Waymo opened a new front in its robotaxi expansion this week, launching fully driverless pickups and drop-offs at Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas. The service begins with riders who already have accounts in the city, Waymo's existing foothold since 2023, before widening to the rest of its Las Vegas service area. It is a notable milestone: airports are dense, rule-bound environments with curbside cops, honking shuttles, and travelers dragging roller bags into traffic, and Waymo has approached them cautiously elsewhere. Getting an autonomous vehicle fleet woven into that chaos is less a victory lap than a stress test the company clearly believes it can pass.

Eight hundred miles northeast, in Denver, the reception has been less cordial. Six weeks after Waymo's cars started roaming the city, some residents have taken to lobbing eggs at them, a juvenile but pointed form of protest that nonetheless signals real unease. It's not a boycott or a lawsuit — it's yolk on a sensor housing — but it captures something harder to legislate against: a segment of the public that simply does not want these vehicles on their streets, and has decided that civic disobedience looks like a dozen eggs from the grocery store.

Together, the two stories say something about where Waymo actually stands in its national rollout: simultaneously ascending and contested, often in the same news cycle. The Las Vegas airport launch matters commercially because airport runs are lucrative, predictable, and visible — exactly the kind of use case that proves autonomous vehicles can handle institutional infrastructure, not just suburban errands. If Waymo can reliably slot into an airport's curb management system, it builds a template other airports, and other cities weighing whether to let robotaxis in, will study closely.

The Denver egging, by contrast, is a reminder that technological capability and public consent are not the same thing. Robotaxis have driven anxieties in other cities too — about jobs, about safety, about who gets consulted before a company reshapes a street corner — and Denver's episode is a small, sticky expression of that anxiety. It costs Waymo very little in material terms; a windshield wash solves it. But it's also free data on sentiment, the kind that doesn't show up in a safety report or a quarterly earnings call.

For the robotics and autonomous-vehicle industry more broadly, the juxtaposition is instructive. Expansion into critical infrastructure like airports signals that regulators and institutional partners are willing to extend trust. Eggs on a windshield signal that trust from the sidewalk is not automatic, and has to be earned block by block, city by city. Waymo's path forward likely runs through both kinds of proving grounds at once — the formal, infrastructural one, and the messier, more human one where someone just doesn't like having a driverless car idling outside their house.
