Waymo's Fleet Footprint Draws Local Scrutiny

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-20

In Las Vegas, electric-vehicle owners have started noticing a pattern at public charging stations: the plug they need is already occupied, usually by a white Jaguar I-Pace with a spinning sensor dome on its roof. Local drivers tell KTNV that Waymo's robotaxis have become fixtures at shared chargers around the city, sometimes for stretches long enough to make ordinary EV owners wonder if the infrastructure they helped lobby for was ever really meant for them. Waymo began commercial rides in Las Vegas this year as part of its broader march beyond San Francisco and Phoenix, but the complaints suggest that a fleet built for scale is now running into the physical limits of a city that wasn't built for it.

Eight hundred miles away, in a lot whose location has not been made public, investor Ross Gerber says he found something stranger: hundreds of Waymo vehicles sitting idle, parked in neat rows like planes grounded by weather. Gerber, who holds Waymo-adjacent investments through Alphabet, posted about the discovery as evidence that "something crazy is about to happen" — a fleet staged and waiting, he suggested, for an expansion Waymo hasn't yet announced. Waymo has not explained the lot, and Gerber's account is speculative, but the image itself is a useful one: a robotaxi company that talks about "learning" and "safety data" is also, at bottom, an operator of a very large and very physical vehicle fleet that has to be charged, stored, cleaned, and parked somewhere.

That physical reality is the real story here. Waymo has spent years positioning itself as a software company with a driving problem solved — the glamorous part is the AI, the sensors, the millions of simulated miles. But a fleet of hundreds or thousands of EVs needs chargers, and chargers are a finite public resource that cities have spent a decade building out, largely with the assumption that they'd serve individual car owners topping up overnight. When a well-capitalized company shows up and treats those same chargers as fleet infrastructure, the math changes fast, and so does the politics. Las Vegas's grumbling drivers are an early, small-scale preview of a fight that other cities — Phoenix, Los Angeles, Austin — will likely have too, over who gets priority on curb space, charging bays, and parking lots as robotaxi fleets grow.

It also reframes what "scale" means for autonomous vehicles. The industry's public narrative has been about miles driven and safety records; the quieter, less-discussed constraint is logistics — where do you park a thousand robotaxis when demand dips, and how do you keep them charged without starving the public grid meant for everyone else? Gerber's mystery lot and Las Vegas's crowded chargers are really the same story, seen from two ends: a company whose ambitions are outrunning the unglamorous infrastructure required to support them, in cities that are only now starting to notice.
