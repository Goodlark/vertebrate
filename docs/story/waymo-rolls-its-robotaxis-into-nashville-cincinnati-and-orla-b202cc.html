Waymo Rolls Its Robotaxis Into Nashville, Cincinnati and Orlando

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-28

Waymo this week pushed its driverless footprint deeper into the American South and Midwest, expanding its Nashville service zone to include the Nashville Zoo, the Green Hills shopping district and Lipscomb University, preparing a launch in Cincinnati, and rolling its white Jaguar I-PACEs onto the streets of Orlando, where one of them promptly went viral for sitting frozen near Universal Studios. Three cities, three different stages of rollout, and one familiar pattern: the robots arrive before the reassurance does.

The Nashville expansion is the most straightforward of the three — an incremental widening of a service area that already covers much of the city, now stretching to take in a zoo, a mall district and a university campus, the kind of everyday errands that are supposed to make robotaxis feel less like a novelty and more like infrastructure. Cincinnati is earlier in the process, with Waymo vehicles expected on local roads soon and at least one local expert already on record warning about the risks of putting autonomous cars into a city that hasn't yet had to reckon with them. And in Orlando, the launch got its unofficial mascot in the form of a stalled Waymo near one of the country's busiest tourist attractions, captured on video and circulated with the kind of glee usually reserved for self-driving-car mishaps.

None of this is new, exactly — Waymo has been adding cities at a pace that outstrips most people's ability to form an opinion about the last one before the next one arrives. What's notable is the gap the summary points to: the expansion is outrunning public trust. A stuck car in Orlando is a minor glitch, not a safety crisis, but it's exactly the kind of image that sticks, because it confirms a suspicion many people already have — that these vehicles are still, in some fundamental way, guessing. Cincinnati's experts aren't wrong to ask questions before the cars arrive rather than after; it's cheaper, politically and otherwise, to litigate safety in advance than to explain it after an incident.

For the robotaxi industry, the stakes are bigger than any one frozen car. Waymo is the furthest along of any U.S. operator, and its expansion strategy — quietly annexing neighborhoods, then whole metro areas — is the template everyone else, from Cruise's successors to Tesla's robotaxi ambitions, will be measured against. Each new city is a test not just of the technology but of municipal appetite: how much friction local governments, insurers and skeptical drivers will tolerate before the convenience of a car with no one up front starts to feel normal rather than uncanny. The economic promise is real — fewer human drivers, new ride-hailing revenue, a glimpse of cities reorganized around vehicles that don't get tired or distracted. But that promise is still being sold one viral clip, one op-ed, one city council meeting at a time, and right now the clips are doing more work than the data.
