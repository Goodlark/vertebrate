Bridge Neurotech Raises $13.5M, Partners With Butterfly Network on Non-Invasive BCI

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-07

Bridge Neurotech has closed a $13.5 million funding round and announced a partnership with Butterfly Network, the ultrasound imaging company, to develop a brain-computer interface that requires no surgery at all. The two announcements landed together, a signal that the startup sees capital and technical firepower as two halves of the same bet: that the next generation of BCI won't need a neurosurgeon.

The partnership pairs Bridge Neurotech's signal-processing work with Butterfly's ultrasound hardware, aiming to read brain activity through the skull rather than from electrodes implanted beneath it. Terms of the collaboration weren't disclosed, nor was a timeline for a working product, but the ambition is stated plainly: a fully non-invasive system that sidesteps the single biggest obstacle standing between BCI and the mass market.

That obstacle is surgery. Every prominent BCI effort to date — Neuralink's implants, Synchron's stent-based electrodes threaded through blood vessels — has required some form of invasive procedure to get sensors close enough to neurons to pick up a usable signal. That's fine for the clinical trials these companies currently run, mostly in patients with severe paralysis who have little to lose and much to gain. It's a nonstarter for the much larger population of people who might want a BCI to type faster, control a prosthetic, or interface with a computer, but aren't willing to have a hole drilled in their skull to do it.

Ultrasound is one of the few technologies with a plausible shot at closing that gap. It can penetrate bone and soft tissue to sense — and in some experimental setups, stimulate — neural activity without breaking the skin. Butterfly Network built its business miniaturizing ultrasound into a handheld probe that plugs into a smartphone, undercutting room-sized imaging machines in hospitals; applying that same chip-scale approach to the skull is a logical, if difficult, next step. Bridge Neurotech's contribution is presumably the decoding side: turning the faint, noisy signals ultrasound can detect into something resembling intention — a cursor movement, a word, a command.

Whether that's achievable at a resolution useful enough to rival implanted electrodes remains an open, and old, question in neurotechnology; signal quality has always been the tradeoff for avoiding surgery. But the money matters regardless of the science's final verdict. BCI has spent years as a story about paralyzed patients and venture-backed moonshots led by Elon Musk's gravitational pull. A credible non-invasive approach, backed by a company that already has FDA-cleared hardware in Butterfly's ultrasound probes, pushes the technology a step closer to something ordinary people might actually use — and pulls the industry's center of gravity away from the operating room and toward the medicine cabinet.
