Tesla Grows Austin Robotaxi Fleet While Skeptics Say Waymo Still Leads

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-04

Tesla this week expanded its robotaxi operation in Austin, adding 111 Cybercabs to the fleet and extending the hours during which the service runs, according to notateslaapp.com. The move, which comes as Tesla also reported stronger-than-expected delivery numbers for the third quarter, is the company's latest attempt to prove that its driverless ambitions in Texas are becoming a real business rather than a pilot project running on borrowed time and investor patience.

The expansion is concrete: more cars on the road, more hours when a rider in Austin can summon one. But it lands alongside a pointed critique from analyst Gary Black, who argues that for all the fleet growth, Tesla is still trailing Waymo in the one metric that actually matters for autonomy — demonstrated capability. Black's argument, reported by Stocktwits, is that Waymo has pulled further ahead on the hard technical problem even as Tesla piles up favorable headlines about deliveries and vehicle counts.

The disagreement isn't really about whether Tesla is growing. It plainly is. The question is what kind of growth counts. Tesla's story is told in units: Cybercabs deployed, hours of service, cars on a map. Waymo's story is told in a different currency entirely — miles driven with nobody touching the wheel, a number that has become the industry's de facto proof of trust. One company is scaling a fleet; the other, by this account, is scaling a technology. Those are not the same achievement, even if from the sidewalk they can look identical — a car pulling up with no one in the driver's seat.

Why this matters extends well beyond Austin traffic. Robotaxis are the clearest commercial test of whether self-driving technology is ready for ordinary people, not just engineers with safety drivers and clipboards. Investors are using fleet size as a proxy for progress because it's legible — a number you can count from a press release. But regulators, insurers, and the riders themselves will eventually care about something harder to spin: how the cars behave in the unpredictable seconds of real driving, the pedestrian who steps off a curb a beat too soon. If Tesla's expansion outpaces its actual autonomous reliability, the gap could surface publicly — in an incident, a regulatory inquiry, or simply in rider experience — in a way that no press release about fleet size can soften.

For the broader robotics and autonomous vehicle industry, the episode is a reminder that scale and sophistication can diverge, at least for a while. Tesla's bet is that deploying more vehicles, faster, builds the data and public familiarity needed to close any capability gap. Waymo's bet, implicit in its slower, mileage-first approach, is that trust is earned car by car, mile by mitigated mile, and that shortcuts show up eventually. Which bet wins will shape not just two companies' stock prices, but how quickly the rest of the country gets comfortable sharing the road with a car that has no one driving it at all.
