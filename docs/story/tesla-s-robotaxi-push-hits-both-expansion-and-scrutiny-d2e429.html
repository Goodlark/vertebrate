Tesla's Robotaxi Push Hits Both Expansion and Scrutiny

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-05

Tesla is building out its robotaxi business on two fronts at once, and the two fronts tell almost opposite stories. In San Antonio, city officials this week approved Tesla's plan to build a robotaxi hub near the airport, giving the company a staging ground for its driverless fleet in a second major Texas market. Meanwhile in Austin, where Tesla's Cybercab has already been shuttling passengers through downtown traffic, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has opened a formal safety investigation into how the vehicle has performed since its rollout.

The San Antonio approval is the kind of unglamorous municipal decision that rarely makes headlines but matters enormously to a company trying to scale a fleet business: a physical hub near an airport means a staging lot, charging infrastructure, and a base from which cars can be dispatched to riders. It's the plumbing beneath the app, and Tesla now has it in two Texas cities rather than one.

But the federal probe in Austin is the harder story, and the one that will likely shape how fast Tesla can move everywhere else. NHTSA doesn't open investigations lightly, and a driverless-vehicle inquiry so soon after a public rollout suggests the agency saw something in Austin's early data worth examining closely — not necessarily a smoking gun, but enough anomalies or complaints to warrant a formal look rather than informal monitoring.

For Tesla, the timing is awkward but not unfamiliar. The company has built its entire public identity around moving fast on autonomy — promising robotaxis for years before delivering a limited, geofenced version in Austin — and it has just as consistently drawn regulatory attention for doing so. What's different now is that the expansion and the scrutiny are happening in the same news cycle, almost as if to underline that they're two sides of the same bet: grow the fleet quickly to prove the business model, while regulators try to determine whether the technology deserves the trust it's asking for.

The stakes extend well beyond Tesla's stock price. Robotaxis are the first mass-market test of whether Americans will actually ride in cars with no one at the wheel, and San Antonio and Austin are becoming the country's proving grounds for that question. If the NHTSA probe surfaces real safety problems, it could slow not just Tesla's rollout but the broader industry's case that driverless fleets are ready for city streets. If it clears the Cybercab, Tesla gains momentum right as it's laying literal groundwork — hubs, lots, charging bays — for the next cities in line. Either way, the fight over robotaxis is no longer happening in investor presentations. It's happening in city council chambers and federal case files, which is exactly where an industry this consequential belongs.
