Uber Presses Ahead on Robotaxis, Even as Lucid-Nuro Launch Slips Again

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-04

Uber is making its most aggressive move yet into robotaxis, telling the Financial Times it intends to have more than 2,000 driverless vehicles operating on its platform, a number that would mark a decisive shift from the cautious, test-the-waters posture the company has maintained since it sold off its own self-driving unit, Uber ATG, back in 2020. At the same time, one of the marquee partnerships meant to help get there has hit yet another pothole: the Gravity robotaxi, built by Lucid and outfitted with Nuro's autonomous technology, has slipped again, this time to the first quarter of 2027.

The delay is not a new story so much as a familiar one with a later date stamped on it. The Lucid-Nuro robotaxi has been pushed back repeatedly since it was first announced, and the latest postponement underscores how stubbornly difficult it remains to move from demo laps to commercial deployment, even for well-funded partners with a luxury EV and a self-driving specialist that has spent years refining its technology for low-speed, local delivery and ride-hailing use cases.

That contrast — Uber talking boldly about scale while one of its own autonomous bets languishes — captures where the robotaxi industry actually stands in late 2025. Waymo has quietly become the dominant presence on American streets, racking up paid rides in multiple cities, while Tesla's robotaxi ambitions remain mostly promises. Uber's strategy has never been to build the technology itself; instead, it has positioned its app as the marketplace where various autonomous-vehicle makers — Waymo, Nuro, Aurora, WeRide, and others — can find riders. The 2,000-vehicle target suggests Uber believes that aggregation model is finally ready to scale, even if any single partner's hardware isn't.

For the broader robotics and automotive industries, the stakes are real. Every slipped launch date is a reminder that autonomous driving remains an unforgiving engineering problem, one where regulatory approval, sensor reliability, and plain old safety validation can eat years of runway that press releases promised would be months. But Uber's willingness to keep pressing forward, publicly setting numerical targets rather than vague aspirations, signals that the ride-hailing giant sees driverless cars not as a distant moonshot but as a near-term operational reality it needs to be ready for.

For riders, the practical effect of 2,000 driverless vehicles would be modest at first — a fraction of Uber's millions of daily trips — but symbolically significant, normalizing the experience of climbing into a car with no one behind the wheel. For investors and automakers watching Lucid, the Gravity delay is a costly reminder that building an EV is one challenge; teaching it to drive itself, reliably, at commercial scale, is another one entirely, and the clock keeps resetting.
