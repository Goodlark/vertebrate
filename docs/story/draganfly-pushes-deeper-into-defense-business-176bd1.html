Draganfly Pushes Deeper Into Defense Business

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-25

Draganfly, the Saskatoon-based drone maker, announced two moves this week aimed at deepening its foothold in the defense sector. The company took a strategic stake in DoD Solution, an Estonian-Ukrainian firm that builds GPS-denied navigation systems for unmanned vehicles, with Draganfly CEO Cameron Chell joining DoD Solution's board. Separately, Draganfly formed a dedicated U.S. defense subsidiary and installed Thomas Modly, the former acting Secretary of the Navy, as its chairman.

The two announcements, coming in quick succession, read less like coincidence than strategy. DoD Solution's expertise lies in keeping drones flying when GPS signals are jammed or spoofed — a problem that has become central to warfare in Ukraine, where electronic warfare units on both sides routinely blind the satellite-guided systems that once made drone navigation simple. By taking a stake in the company and putting its own CEO on the board, Draganfly is buying both technology and a front-row seat in a market shaped daily by combat conditions rather than lab simulations.

The U.S. subsidiary, meanwhile, is a more conventional play for access. Modly's résumé — acting Navy secretary, Pentagon insider — is the kind of credential that opens doors in Washington procurement offices that remain, for all the Pentagon's talk of innovation, stubbornly difficult for smaller drone makers to walk through. His chairmanship signals that Draganfly wants to be taken seriously not just as a vendor of hardware but as a company capable of navigating the defense acquisition bureaucracy.

Together, the moves mark a clear pivot for a company that built its early reputation on public-safety and commercial drones — search-and-rescue missions, agricultural surveying — toward the more lucrative, more geopolitically charged business of military autonomy. It's a shift mirrored across the drone industry broadly, as companies from Anduril to smaller players recalibrate around the lessons of Ukraine, where cheap, resilient, jam-resistant systems have proven more valuable than expensive, exquisite ones.

For the broader robotics industry, the symbolism matters as much as the specifics. Draganfly is a relatively modest player, but its moves track a larger reordering of the sector: autonomy companies increasingly treat the Ukrainian battlefield as the proving ground that determines which technologies get funded, acquired, and scaled. GPS-denial resilience, once a niche military concern, is quickly becoming table stakes for any serious drone manufacturer, with implications that will eventually filter down to commercial and public-safety applications as well.

And for the Pentagon, which has struggled to court nimble, mid-sized firms, hires like Modly's and investments like Draganfly's suggest a steady institutional magnetism — fading the old line between Silicon Valley's drone startups and the traditional defense establishment that still controls the money.
