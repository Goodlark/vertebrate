Waymo's Expansion Hits Local Speed Bumps in St. Paul and Philadelphia

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-09

Waymo's autonomous vehicles are now familiar enough in Phoenix and San Francisco that they barely register as news. That is not the case in St. Paul or Philadelphia. This week, the company's expansion into both cities produced not fanfare but friction: in St. Paul, the city council president is pushing for formal oversight rules before Waymo's vehicles become a routine part of the streetscape, and in Philadelphia, after more than a year of on-road testing, the company still will not say when — or whether — it plans to remove the human safety drivers currently riding shotgun.

The Philadelphia testing program has run long enough that its ambiguity has become its own kind of answer. Waymo has operated there for upward of a year, accumulating the miles and data that typically precede a driverless launch elsewhere. But when PhillyVoice asked for a timeline, the company offered none. No date, no season, no milestone to watch for. That silence reads differently than it might have a few years ago, when Waymo was racing to prove the technology worked. Now the technology mostly isn't the question — the politics and logistics of a new city are.

In St. Paul, the conversation hasn't even reached the question of driverless operation. The city council president is asking for rules to govern how Waymo operates at all — a request that implies the city currently has little formal say in the matter, and would like to change that before the vehicles become entrenched. It's a preemptive move, the kind of thing municipal officials do when they've watched how these rollouts played out elsewhere and want a say before, rather than after, the cars are everywhere.

Taken together, the two stories complicate the narrative Waymo has built in its home markets, where the robotaxis have become something close to civic furniture. California and Arizona got years of iterative buildup, regulatory relationships built over time, and a public that grew accustomed to the vehicles gradually. St. Paul and Philadelphia are getting a company that has already proven the core technology and is now negotiating, city by city, for the social license to deploy it. That negotiation is turning out to be slower and more contested than the engineering ever was.

This matters beyond Waymo's quarterly expansion map. The company is the furthest along of any robotaxi operator in the country, and its experience is the template other cities will reference when autonomous vehicles come knocking. A council president demanding oversight rules in St. Paul, or a company declining to commit to a timeline in Philadelphia, signals that the easy wins — the cities eager to be first — are behind the industry. What's ahead is the harder, more typical American city: skeptical, procedural, and in no hurry to hand over its streets without conditions attached. For an industry whose entire pitch rests on scale, that slower cadence is the real story.
