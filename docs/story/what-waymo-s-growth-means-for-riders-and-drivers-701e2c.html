What Waymo's Growth Means for Riders and Drivers

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-28

Waymo's robotaxis are no longer a novelty confined to the sun-baked grids of Phoenix. The Alphabet-owned company has been widening its footprint across American cities and, more recently, signaling plans to cross into Toronto, a move that has set off two very different conversations about what happens when a car with no driver becomes a normal part of getting around. One, raised this week by WUSF, asks whether the technology might finally solve a transportation problem that has vexed disabled riders for decades. The other, from CBC, asks what happens to the humans currently behind the wheel.

The accessibility case is not abstract. For people who use wheelchairs, who are blind, or who have conditions that make driving impossible, the ride-hail economy has often been a story of broken promises — drivers who cancel upon seeing a folded wheelchair, apps that assume a trunk will fit a scooter, the general awkwardness of needing a stranger's goodwill to get to a doctor's appointment. A car that never gets impatient, never discriminates, and never speeds off because it doesn't want to deal with a service animal is, on paper, a quietly radical proposition. WUSF's essay treats this as a live question rather than a settled fact, and rightly so: Waymo's vehicles still require sidewalks to pull up to, apps to navigate, and infrastructure that wasn't built with every disability in mind. But the underlying promise — consistency, patience, and a lack of prejudice — is one that human-driven services have struggled to deliver at scale.

North of the border, the calculus looks different. CBC's reporting on Toronto frames Waymo's expansion as a direct threat to the city's taxi and Uber drivers, many of whom are immigrants for whom driving has long served as an accessible entry point into the economy. A fleet of software-driven cars that never needs a paycheck, a bathroom break, or a union contract is an efficient machine for a company's balance sheet and a potentially brutal one for a workforce that has already absorbed one disruption, from the arrival of ride-hailing apps themselves, with limited consequence awarded to them.

Taken together, these two stories outline the central tension of the robotaxi era: the same technology that could extend independence to people who've been failed by existing transit can simultaneously hollow out one of the more accessible jobs available to people without other options. Regulators in Toronto, as in many cities now watching Waymo's expansion with a mix of curiosity and anxiety, will have to decide not whether this technology works, but who it is allowed to work for, and at whose expense it is allowed to succeed.
