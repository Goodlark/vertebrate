Musk Talks Up Tesla's Robotaxi Numbers While Waymo Keeps Driving More Miles

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-27

Elon Musk spent part of this week doing what he does best: declaring victory in a race that, by most measures, he is still losing. In a string of posts, the Tesla chief pointed to a single strong week of robotaxi rides in Austin — one that, he noted, topped Waymo's average weekly volume in the second quarter — and to a Texas fleet that has now crossed 1,000 vehicles, with the boxy, driverless Cybercab outnumbering the Model Y for the first time in Tesla's active robotaxi lineup. The timing was no accident. Tesla has been eager to show that its long-delayed robotaxi bet is finally accelerating beyond the handful of supervised cars that launched in Austin this past summer.

The trouble with Musk's victory lap is the denominator. Waymo, the Alphabet-owned company that has been quietly accumulating driverless miles in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles and beyond since long before Tesla put a single Cybercab on the road, still logs roughly seven times as many autonomous miles as Tesla's entire robotaxi operation. A single flashy week in one Texas city, however real, doesn't erase a multiyear head start measured in the hundreds of millions of miles. Musk's math is technically accurate and strategically selective — the rhetorical equivalent of bragging about a strong quarter while ignoring the balance sheet.

That gap matters beyond bragging rights. Autonomous driving is one of those technologies where cumulative miles aren't just a vanity metric — they're the raw material that trains and validates the software, exposing it to the weird edge cases (a shopping cart in traffic, a cyclist doing something unwise) that only show up at scale. Waymo's mileage advantage reflects years of methodical expansion under less public scrutiny, while Tesla has leaned on Musk's gift for narrative to suggest it's closing a gap that the underlying data doesn't yet support.

For the robotics and autonomous-vehicle industry, the dueling claims are a reminder that this market is still being fought as much in public perception as in parking lots. Investors watching Tesla's stock price care about momentum narratives; regulators and city officials in Austin, Phoenix and elsewhere care about safety records built over millions of real-world miles. The Cybercab's rise within Tesla's own fleet signals the company is serious about a purpose-built robotaxi rather than a retrofitted sedan, which is a genuine milestone. But for ordinary riders deciding whether to trust a driverless car with their commute, the more relevant number isn't who had the better week — it's who has driven the most miles without anyone getting hurt. On that score, Waymo's quiet accumulation still speaks louder than Musk's megaphone.
