Tesla Stock Slides as EU Delays Full Self-Driving Vote to December 15

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-25

Brussels did nothing on Tuesday, and that was enough to knock billions off Tesla's market value. European regulators postponed, for the second time, a vote on approving the company's Full Self-Driving software for use on the continent's roads, resetting the decision date to December 15. The delay, first reported by Electrek, gave markets little to chew on but uncertainty — and uncertainty, where Tesla is concerned, has become its own kind of catalyst. Shares fell 6% on the news, according to 24/7 Wall St., with the selloff spilling over into fellow electric-vehicle makers Rivian and Lucid, both down 4% in sympathy.

The EU has not explained its reasoning for the second postponement, and that silence is doing a lot of work. For a stock whose valuation leans heavily on the promise of autonomy — robotaxis, licensing deals, a future where Tesla sells software margins instead of sheet metal — a regulatory body simply declining to say yes or no is its own kind of verdict. Investors have spent years pricing in Elon Musk's timelines for full autonomy; they have grown considerably less patient with Europe's.

The stakes here extend well beyond one company's quarterly share price. Full Self-Driving is the clearest test case the industry has for how governments will actually regulate autonomous vehicles once the marketing copy meets the pavement. The EU's bureaucratic caution — this is now the second delay — signals that European regulators are not going to be rushed by Silicon Valley product cycles or by a Tesla earnings call. That matters for every robotics and AV company eyeing the European market, because the same scrutiny applied to Tesla will almost certainly apply to Waymo, Mobileye, and whoever comes next. A slow, deliberate approval process is, in one sense, good news for public safety; it is also a drag on an industry that has promised investors speed.

The sympathy drop in Rivian and Lucid shares is its own small data point about how thin the line has become between "EV company" and "autonomy company" in the market's imagination. Neither Rivian nor Lucid has anything riding on this particular EU vote, yet their stocks moved anyway, which suggests investors are trading the sector as a single basket pegged to the idea that self-driving technology is the next profit frontier. When that idea wobbles, everyone's stock wobbles with it.

For ordinary drivers, the practical consequence is simpler: another six weeks, at minimum, before European regulators say anything official about whether Tesla's software is fit to operate with greater autonomy on their roads. For the industry, the consequence is a reminder that robotics and AI companies are now subject to the same forces that move oil or semiconductor stocks — a single bureaucratic calendar entry, oceans away, can erase billions in value before lunch.
