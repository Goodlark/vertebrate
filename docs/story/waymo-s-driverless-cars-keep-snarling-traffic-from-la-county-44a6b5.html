Waymo's Driverless Cars Keep Snarling Traffic, From LA County to Orlando

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-29

In the span of a week, Waymo's driverless cars managed to stall traffic in two very different corners of the country. Near Universal Orlando in Florida, one of the company's autonomous Jaguars came to a dead stop on a narrow, one-lane access road, backing up the kind of theme-park traffic that tests the patience of even the most sedate vacationer. Meanwhile, in a city in Los Angeles County, complaints about Waymo vehicles have grown persistent enough that local police have stepped up patrols just to manage the friction the cars are creating on city streets.

Neither incident, on its own, is catastrophic. No one was hurt at Universal Orlando; the car simply sat there, an expensive paperweight on wheels, while the vacationers behind it presumably contemplated the gap between Silicon Valley's promises and the realities of a tourist bottleneck. In Los Angeles County, the complaints that prompted the police response haven't been detailed as a single dramatic failure but as an accumulation of smaller ones — the sort of low-grade nuisance that doesn't make headlines individually but adds up to a city deciding it needs more officers on the street to referee the problem.

Taken together, though, the two episodes say something larger about where Waymo's technology actually stands. For years, the company's public narrative has centered on safety statistics and millions of rider-only miles, numbers meant to reassure skeptics that the hard part — the testing, the edge cases, the long tail of weird situations — was being handled in a controlled, methodical way. What's showing up now in Florida and California is a different kind of data point: the mundane friction of robots trying to coexist with human traffic patterns in places the test fleets weren't necessarily built for, like a theme park access road choked with tourists, or a Los Angeles County suburb where everyday drivers are apparently fed up enough to call the police.

That friction matters beyond the inconvenience of a stalled car or an annoyed commuter. Waymo has positioned itself as the autonomous-vehicle industry's most credible player, the one furthest along the path toward normalizing robotaxis as an unremarkable part of urban life. Every stall, every complaint that escalates into a police patrol, chips away at that narrative and hands ammunition to regulators and skeptics who've argued all along that the technology is being deployed faster than it's being perfected. For the broader robotics and self-driving industry, which is watching Waymo as the bellwether, these are the kinds of stories that shape public appetite — and public appetite, more than any engineering milestone, will determine how quickly driverless cars actually become a fixture on American streets rather than a curiosity that occasionally brings traffic to a halt.
