Waymo Faces Growing Scrutiny Over Where It's Headed and How Fast

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-05

Waymo's robotaxis are, at this point, a familiar sight on the streets of Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles and Austin, gliding along with the mild confidence of a chauffeur who has never once gotten lost. This month, that confidence has run up against a more skeptical audience: newspaper editorial boards and columnists asking, in effect, where exactly this is all going. The Wall Street Journal published a column that posed the question almost plaintively — "Where are you taking me, Waymo?" — while the Philadelphia Inquirer's editorial board weighed in on the company's testing period in that city, urging officials not to treat it as a rubber stamp on the way to a foregone conclusion.

Neither piece is a takedown of the technology itself. Waymo's cars remain, by most accounts, remarkably good at the narrow task of driving without hitting things. What's changed is the tenor of the conversation around them, as the company expands from a handful of Sunbelt cities into denser, older, more chaotic urban environments — Philadelphia among them — where the margin for error narrows and the stakes of getting it wrong rise accordingly. The Journal's columnist's question wasn't really about navigation; it was about ambition, about what Waymo and its corporate parent, Alphabet, actually want this network of driverless cars to become, and whether the public has been given an honest account of that.

The Inquirer's argument was more procedural, and in some ways more pointed: testing periods, it warned, have a way of becoming theater when everyone involved — the company, the city, the press — has already decided how the story ends. Its editorial board asked Philadelphia's officials to actually use the evaluation window in front of them, rather than nodding it through as a formality before Waymo's inevitable arrival in force.

That shift in mood matters beyond the opinion pages. For years, the dominant narrative around autonomous vehicles was technological — could the cars drive themselves reliably? Increasingly, the questions being asked are civic and economic: who decides which streets get robotaxis, what happens to human drivers displaced by them, and who is accountable when something goes wrong at scale rather than in a single, containable incident. Waymo has largely won the argument about capability. What it hasn't yet fully won is the argument about governance — about whether the pace of its expansion is being set by public deliberation or by corporate momentum. As more cities inherit the same choice Philadelphia now faces, that distinction is likely to matter more than any single self-driving mile logged.
