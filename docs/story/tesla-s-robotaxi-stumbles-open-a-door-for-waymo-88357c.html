Tesla's Robotaxi Stumbles Open a Door for Waymo

Compiled by machine · 2026-10-15

In Austin this month, the gap between Tesla's robotaxi pitch and its practice widened into something harder to ignore. Data compiled from the company's limited robotaxi service shows vehicles operating under remote human control — not the full autonomy Tesla has spent a decade promising — are getting into crashes at a rate that undercuts the safety narrative Elon Musk has built the program's valuation on. Electrek, which has tracked the service since its June launch, reports the pattern is frequent enough to suggest teleoperation is doing more of the driving than Tesla lets on.

That distinction matters because Tesla's entire robotaxi premise rests on the idea that its cars see and think for themselves, cheaply, at scale, without a human in a distant office nudging a joystick when the cameras get confused by Texas sun glare or a stalled pickup truck. A remote operator is not a trivial backstop; it's a admission that the software isn't yet trustworthy enough to leave alone. And remote driving, it turns out, carries its own failure modes — latency, degraded video feeds, an operator managing multiple cars at once — that are now showing up as fender-benders and worse in the crash logs.

Meanwhile, on the financial-commentary side, 24/7 Wall St. floated a scenario that Tesla bulls would rather not dwell on: that Alphabet, sitting on Waymo's decade-plus head start in genuine driverless operation, could simply price Tesla out of whatever "first mover" premium it hoped to charge. There's no announced fare war, no new city rollout behind this — it's speculation, not strategy. But it's speculation grounded in an asymmetry that's becoming harder to paper over: Waymo has been running rides with no one behind the wheel, in real traffic, in multiple cities, for years, while Tesla's Austin fleet still leans on a remote human safety net.

The stakes extend well beyond two companies' stock charts. Robotaxis are being sold to the public as a solution to drunk driving, traffic fatalities, the tedium of urban congestion — a technology meant to be trusted precisely because it removes human error from the loop. If Tesla's version is quietly human-assisted and still crashing, that's not just a competitive problem, it's a credibility problem for the whole category, at a moment when regulators and insurers are still deciding how much latitude to give any of these fleets. Waymo's methodical, geofenced approach has always looked slow next to Musk's promises of robotaxis blanketing America by now. But slow, it turns out, may be what keeps the cars — and the company's premium — intact.
