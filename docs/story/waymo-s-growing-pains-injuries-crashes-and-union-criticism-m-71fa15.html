Waymo's Growing Pains: Injuries, Crashes, and Union Criticism Mount

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-28

This week brought a small avalanche of bad headlines for Waymo, the Alphabet-owned robotaxi company that has spent the past year racing to expand its driverless fleet across American cities. The Transport Workers Union published a newsletter titled "Coast-to-Coast Waymo Woes," a pointed catalogue of grievances against the company's operations nationwide. Separately, TechCrunch reported that test drivers employed by Waymo and rival Zoox are turning up with whiplash and sprains as the companies push to scale robotaxi deployments. And on the road, the incidents kept coming: a Waymo vehicle was struck in a collision in Denver, according to local station 9News, while another Waymo collided with a minivan outside a Costco in California — a fender-bender that, as Yahoo Autos felt compelled to clarify, was not a hit-and-run.

None of these items, taken alone, would make much news. A crash outside a Costco is the kind of thing that happens in every parking lot in America every day. But taken together, they sketch a pattern that matters: the friction of scaling a technology that was sold to the public as safer than human driving, and to workers as a clean break from the grind of the job it's meant to replace.

The union's complaint and the test-driver injuries point to a less-discussed layer of the robotaxi business — the humans still required to make the machines work. Test drivers, who monitor and sometimes intervene in vehicles that are not yet fully autonomous, are reporting the kind of soft-tissue injuries associated with sudden braking and evasive maneuvers, the physical toll of babysitting a system still learning the difference between a shopping cart and a child. That's a labor story as much as a technology one, and it complicates the narrative that autonomous vehicles simply subtract human risk from the roads.

The crashes themselves are more ambiguous. Waymo has long touted safety data suggesting its vehicles get into fewer serious collisions than human-driven cars over comparable mileage, and a robotaxi getting rear-ended in Denver or clipped by a minivan near a Costco doesn't necessarily indict the software — plenty of these incidents look like ordinary bad luck involving other drivers. But each crash report chips away at the public's patience for the visible mess of that rollout, especially in cities where Waymo is still asking regulators and residents for the benefit of the doubt.

What's emerging is not proof that robotaxis are unsafe, but evidence that the industry's move-fast expansion carries costs — to workers' bodies, to public trust, to the tidy narrative that autonomy arrives without friction. For an industry that has staked its legitimacy on being demonstrably better than the status quo, a steady drip of union newsletters and fender-benders is its own kind of hazard, one measured not in insurance claims but in the slow erosion of the benefit of the doubt.
