Uber Leans Into Driverless Rides, Starting With Zoox in Las Vegas

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-16

Uber began offering rides this week in Amazon’s Zoox robotaxi in Las Vegas, the first time the boxy, windowless-looking vehicle has carried paying passengers outside Amazon’s own supervision. The arrangement, confirmed by Bloomberg, threads Zoox’s hardware through Uber’s app, meaning a rider summoning a car on the Strip might now find something with no steering wheel pulling up at the curb. A reporter for USA Today took one of the rides and, in the time-honored tradition of journalists testing the future, lived to describe it — a bit bemused, mostly reassured, the ordinary texture of a cab ride rendered faintly uncanny by the absence of anyone up front to blame for the traffic.

The Las Vegas launch is small in scale but large in symbolism. Zoox, which Amazon bought in 2020 for more than a billion dollars, has spent years building a vehicle from scratch — no pedals, seats facing each other, designed for a world where nobody drives. Until now, it has mostly ferried Amazon employees and invited guests in closed pilots. Routing it through Uber's app changes the calculus: instead of asking people to seek out a novelty, Uber is quietly inserting autonomy into an app already on thirty million phones, letting the habit of hailing a ride do the work of acclimating people to a car with nobody in it.

That matters because the real obstacle to robotaxis has never been purely technical. Waymo, Cruise, and now Zoox have all demonstrated that the driving itself is largely solvable in constrained geographies. The harder problem is psychological and commercial: convincing millions of ordinary riders, not just tech reporters and investors, that an empty driver’s seat is unremarkable, and building the fleet logistics to make that convenience available at the scale Uber already operates at. By becoming the distribution layer for Zoox — as it has for Waymo in Phoenix and Austin — Uber is positioning itself not as a company racing to build its own self-driving car, but as the marketplace that decides which robots get hailed, a far less capital-intensive bet than the one it abandoned when it sold its own self-driving unit in 2020.

For the robotics industry, the deal is a reminder that the economics of autonomy are consolidating around partnerships rather than vertical empires. For riders in Las Vegas, and eventually other cities, it means the strange becoming mundane a little faster: a car that drives itself, summoned the same way you’d summon any other, its novelty wearing off somewhere between the casino and the airport.
