Waymo Expands Footprint to Sacramento and Munich

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-25

California regulators have cleared Waymo to begin driverless operations on the streets of Sacramento, the company confirmed this week, adding the capital city to a service map that already includes San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Phoenix. The approval came even as local officials in Sacramento continue to raise questions about street safety, a debate that has trailed Waymo into nearly every city it has entered. Thousands of miles away, in Munich, the company announced a very different kind of first step: it will begin mapping roads and running test drives with human safety operators behind the wheel, with an eye toward a commercial robotaxi launch by the end of 2027. It is Waymo's first venture outside the United States.

The two announcements, arriving together, tell a story about a company trying to prove two things at once — that it can deepen its presence in the markets where it already operates, and that its technology can travel. Sacramento is a logical next domestic stop: a mid-sized capital with grid-like downtown streets not unlike the terrain Waymo has already mastered in Phoenix and parts of San Francisco. Munich is something else entirely — a test of whether Waymo's geofenced, sensor-heavy approach, built and refined on American roads with American traffic laws and American driving habits, can be transplanted into a European city with narrower streets, different signage, and a regulatory apparatus that has not yet been asked to bless a robotaxi fleet.

For the robotaxi industry, the Munich move matters more than another U.S. city might. Waymo has spent a decade proving out its model incrementally, city by city, inside the relatively permissive regulatory environment of the United States. Europe has been warier of autonomous vehicles, and Germany in particular has a car culture — and a car industry — that will watch closely how a Silicon Valley company navigates its roads and its politics. A successful Munich launch would hand Waymo a template for expansion into other global cities; a stumble would be read as evidence that the geofenced approach doesn't generalize as easily as its backers claim.

Closer to home, the Sacramento rollout is a reminder that scale doesn't erase scrutiny. Even in cities where Waymo has operated for years, local officials and residents continue to ask basic questions about how the cars behave around cyclists, pedestrians, and emergency vehicles — questions that regulators in Sacramento are asking now, before the fleet has logged meaningful mileage there. That tension, between a company eager to expand its footprint and a public still calibrating its trust, is likely to follow Waymo across the Atlantic as well. The company is, in effect, running two experiments simultaneously: one in whether Americans will keep accepting driverless cars as an unremarkable part of city life, and another in whether the rest of the world will let it try.
