Waymo's Driverless Cars Feel Ordinary in the Cabin, Unsettling on the Sidewalk

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-29

The car arrived precisely on time, idled at the curb for exactly as long as the app promised, and pulled away with the kind of unhurried competence that would be unremarkable in a human driver and is, for now, still a small marvel in a machine. This was a Waymo robotaxi in San Francisco, one of several thousand now logging paid rides across the city, and a reporter who climbed into the back seat this week came away with a verdict that doubles as the company's whole pitch: it was boring. The car played music if asked. It did not ask about the reporter's day. It signaled early, merged smoothly, and delivered its passenger to the destination without incident — the automotive equivalent of a well-trained butler who knows when to disappear.

That the most notable thing about a driverless car ride is its lack of notability marks a real inflection point for Waymo, the Alphabet subsidiary that has spent more than a decade and untold billions teaching software to drive. San Francisco, along with Phoenix and Los Angeles, has become a proving ground where the technology has quietly slipped from novelty into infrastructure — as unremarkable, on good days, as a Muni bus.

But eight hundred miles east, in Denver, the public has not caught up to that shrug. A new poll out this month finds Denver residents deeply wary of the idea of Waymo vehicles navigating their streets, even as the company signals plans to push beyond its coastal strongholds into new metro markets. The skepticism is not a fringe reaction; it reflects a broader pattern that has dogged autonomous vehicles since their earliest test runs — the gap between how safe the technology performs in aggregate and how safe any single rider, or any single pedestrian on the sidewalk, feels in the moment.

That gap matters enormously for an industry that has bet its future on exactly this kind of trust transfer. Waymo's expansion strategy depends on cities saying yes before residents have had the chance to log a hundred boring rides of their own. San Francisco took years of visible testing, public complaints, occasional stalled vehicles blocking intersections, and regulatory fights before the ordinary-ness described in this week's ride became possible. Denver, by contrast, is being asked to extend trust in advance of experience — a much harder sell, and one that explains why polling numbers there look so different from the calm of a San Francisco back seat.

The stakes go beyond one company's rollout map. Robotaxis promise to reshape urban transportation economics — labor costs, insurance models, curb space, even how cities plan for congestion — but only if the public consents to ride along, and only if the sidewalk-level unease documented in cities like Denver eventually gives way to the interior calm Waymo is manufacturing, one unremarkable ride at a time.
