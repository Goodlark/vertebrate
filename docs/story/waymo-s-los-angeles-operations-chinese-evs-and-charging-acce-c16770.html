Waymo's Los Angeles Operations: Chinese EVs and Charging Access

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-17

Waymo has moved 3,200 Chinese-built electric vehicles into Los Angeles to feed its robotaxi fleet, a purchase that would have been financially suicidal for anyone without the company's particular set of exemptions. An ordinary buyer importing the same cars would face tariffs of 127.5 percent — a rate designed, in effect, to keep Chinese EVs off American driveways. Waymo, buying not for personal use but for a commercial fleet under different trade rules, sidestepped that wall entirely. The vehicles are now rolling out across the company's Los Angeles service area.

Up the coast in Santa Monica, a smaller drama played out over something far less exotic than trade policy: parking lots. Waymo had lost overnight access to municipal charging lots in the city, a logistical setback that would have complicated how the company powers its fleet between shifts. This month, according to reporting from the Westside Current, the company negotiated its way back in, restoring overnight charging privileges it had briefly lost.

Neither story involves a single ride, a single passenger, or a single mile of autonomous driving. That's rather the point. Waymo's public narrative runs on sensors, neural networks, and the slow erosion of human suspicion toward a car with no one in the driver's seat. But underneath that narrative sits a much duller set of problems — where do you buy the cars, and where do you plug them in — that a robotaxi company has to solve just like any fleet operator, and that get almost no attention because they don't fit the story anyone wants to tell about the future of transportation.

The tariff maneuver matters because it exposes a seam in American trade policy that most consumers will never get to exploit. The 127.5 percent rate exists to protect domestic and allied automakers from a flood of cheap, well-made Chinese EVs; it has functioned, so far, as a fairly effective wall. Waymo's ability to bring in thousands of those same vehicles for commercial fleet use suggests that the wall has doors in it for companies operating at sufficient scale and with the right structure — a detail that will interest both trade lawyers and Waymo's competitors, some of whom are still sourcing vehicles at retail-adjacent prices.

The Santa Monica charging dispute is a smaller matter, but it points to something robotaxi operators rarely advertise: they are, underneath the software, essentially very demanding fleet-management operations, dependent on municipal infrastructure that was not built with them in mind. A city can grant or revoke overnight lot access the way it might for a delivery company or a taxi fleet, and that leverage — however minor it looks next to a tariff exemption — is a reminder that robotaxi expansion runs through city councils and public works departments as much as through engineering labs. Together, the two episodes suggest that scaling a driverless fleet is less a triumph of algorithms than a slog through customs classifications and parking contracts, the unglamorous plumbing that keeps the marketing copy from ever having to mention where the cars actually come from, or where they sleep.
