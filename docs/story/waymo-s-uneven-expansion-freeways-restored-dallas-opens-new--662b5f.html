Waymo's Uneven Expansion: Freeways Restored, Dallas Opens, New York Stalls

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-04

Waymo resumed freeway service for its self-driving taxis this month after temporarily pulling the vehicles off highways to fix technical failures that had sidelined them, the company confirmed. Around the same time, Waymo opened its Dallas robotaxi service to the general public, ending a waitlist that had swelled to roughly 150,000 people since the company began signing up riders there in February. The two moves, announced within the same stretch of days, mark a return to forward motion for a company that spent much of the year managing setbacks as much as expansion.

The freeway fix matters because highway driving has always been the harder problem for autonomous vehicles — higher speeds, faster merges, less room for the software to hesitate. Waymo had kept its cars off freeways while engineers diagnosed what had gone wrong; putting them back on signals the company believes it has closed that gap, at least well enough to resume commercial rides. Dallas, meanwhile, becomes the latest full-scale market for Waymo, following earlier launches in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles and Austin, and the waitlist number itself is a useful data point: demand for robotaxi rides, in cities where Waymo already operates, appears to outstrip the company's ability to supply them.

But an analysis from the American Enterprise Institute points to the limits of that momentum. New York City remains, in AEI's framing, a place where Waymo is stuck — both in the literal congestion of Manhattan streets and in the regulatory maze that has kept the company from operating there in any meaningful way. New York's density, its taxi and for-hire-vehicle rules, and its historically cautious approach to autonomous vehicles have combined to keep Waymo largely on the sidelines in the country's largest media market and one of its most consequential transportation systems.

That contrast — freeways reopened, a Sun Belt city fully unlocked, and the nation's densest city still closed off — says something about where the robotaxi business actually grows. Waymo's expansion has followed a predictable geography: sprawling, car-dependent, regulation-friendly Western and Southern cities where local governments have been eager to court the technology. New York, with its entrenched taxi industry, powerful municipal bureaucracy and narrow streets, represents a different kind of test, one that has less to do with sensors and software than with politics and incumbency.

For the robotaxi industry broadly, the lesson is that technical maturity and market access are separate battles. Waymo can solve a freeway bug in weeks; it cannot, on its own timeline, solve New York City. As other companies watch how this plays out, the pattern emerging is one of uneven geography — a technology that scales quickly where the rules bend, and stalls precisely where the rides, and the symbolism of cracking Manhattan, would matter most.
