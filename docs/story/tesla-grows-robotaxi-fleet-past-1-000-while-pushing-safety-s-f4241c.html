Tesla Grows Robotaxi Fleet Past 1,000 While Pushing Safety Steering to Millions of Cars

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-14

Tesla's driverless ambitions reached a pair of milestones this week, one loud, one quiet. A third-party tracking site that monitors the company's Robotaxi rollout reported that the fleet has now grown past 1,000 vehicles, a threshold reached with little fanfare from Tesla itself, which has kept official numbers scarce since the service launched in Austin. Separately, the company began pushing a software update to millions of vehicles already on the road that allows the car to automatically steer itself out of the way of a potential collision — a capability now available even to owners who never paid for, or opted into, Full Self-Driving.

The two developments, arriving in the same news cycle, aren't officially linked, but they tell a single story about how Tesla is approaching autonomy: expand the flashy, supervised robotaxi service in public view, while extending smaller, semi-autonomous interventions into the ordinary fleet with far less noise. One thousand robotaxis is a modest number against the millions of Teslas on American roads, but it marks a real acceleration from the handful of vehicles that first shuttled riders around Austin. The automatic steering update touches a vastly larger population — owners who bought a Model 3 or Model Y with no intention of using self-driving software now find their car capable of taking evasive action on its own.

For the robotics and automotive industries, the significance lies less in the raw fleet count than in the strategy it reveals. Waymo has scaled its driverless service methodically, city by city, with heavy sensor suites and public regulatory filings. Tesla is doing something different: treating its entire installed base as a rolling test bed for incremental autonomy, deployed through software rather than hardware, and often without customers actively choosing it. That approach lowers the cost of expanding autonomous behavior — no new car needed, just an update — but it also raises the stakes on validation, since the same code now governs steering decisions for drivers who never signed up to be part of an autonomy program.

For everyday drivers, the practical effect is a car that quietly does more without being asked. A steering assist that intervenes to dodge a collision is, on its face, a safety feature, and Tesla has long argued that such automation reduces crashes. But it also blurs a line that regulators and safety researchers have been anxious about for years: the difference between a driver-assist feature and a system that starts making decisions for you. As Tesla pushes further into robotaxi service — with regulators, insurers, and rival companies watching closely — the company is simultaneously normalizing a version of autonomy that arrives in your driveway without a press release, a subscription, or, in many cases, a choice.
