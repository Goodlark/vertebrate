Uber, Nuro and Lucid Start Driverless Testing of Luxury Robotaxi

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-24

Uber, Nuro and Lucid this week began fully driverless testing of their jointly developed robotaxi, pulling safety drivers out from behind the wheel for the first time, the companies said. The vehicle, built on Lucid's Gravity sedan platform and running Nuro's autonomy software, has been navigating public roads without a human backup — a milestone the three partners had been building toward since announcing their alliance last year. Neither the companies nor the reports specified the exact test city, but the move marks the formal start of driverless-only operation ahead of the robotaxi's planned commercial debut.

The timing is pointed. Tesla has spent much of the past year promoting its own robotaxi ambitions, running a limited, closely chaperoned pilot in Austin with employees still riding along as monitors. Lucid and its partners are framing their driverless leap as proof that a quieter, more methodical approach — expensive sensor suites, a dedicated autonomy partner in Nuro, and Uber's logistics muscle — can also get a luxury vehicle onto the road without anyone in the driver's seat.

For Lucid, long better known for high-end electric sedans than for solving the robotaxi puzzle, the test is a chance to reposition itself as an infrastructure supplier to the autonomy boom rather than just another EV maker competing for retail buyers in a soft market. For Nuro, which pivoted away from building its own delivery vehicles to licensing its self-driving stack, the Lucid deal is a vote of confidence that its technology can handle the more complex task of carrying passengers rather than packages. And for Uber, the arrangement fits a now-familiar pattern: rather than build or own a fleet of autonomous vehicles itself, the company is stitching together partnerships — with Waymo, with Nuro and Lucid, and others — so that whichever technology wins, Uber's app is the one booking the ride.

The stakes extend beyond corporate rivalry. Robotaxis without safety drivers represent the regulatory and technical threshold the entire industry has been straining toward for a decade, the point at which autonomous driving stops being a chaperoned demonstration and starts being an actual service. Each company that clears it — Waymo first, Tesla now pushing hard, and Lucid's coalition following — adds pressure on regulators to set consistent rules for how these cars behave at intersections, in bad weather, and when something goes wrong, with no one inside to take over.

It also reshapes what passengers might expect from a robotaxi ride. Where Waymo's Jaguars and Tesla's Model Ys have defined the category so far, a Lucid-based vehicle introduces a luxury tier — leather seats and a quieter cabin — into a market that has mostly sold itself on novelty and convenience. If the driverless tests go smoothly and Uber moves toward the fleet deployment it has signaled interest in, riders in whichever city gets the service first may find that the choice ahead isn't just human driver versus machine, but economy robotaxi versus a plusher one.
