Waymo's Ojai Minivans Go Fully Public, Gain Gemini Brains

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-20

Waymo this week lifted the waitlist on its "Ojai" minivans, the Zeekr-built vehicles that have been quietly shuttling select riders around for months, and opened them to any paying customer with the Waymo app in three cities. The company also confirmed it is threading Google's Gemini model into the vans' software, giving the AI a role that extends past route-mapping into conversational trip planning and the kind of running commentary a human driver might offer, if human drivers were inclined to narrate the freeway on-ramp.

The rollout marks a quiet but telling shift in Waymo's identity. The Jaguar I-PACE, all sharp angles and retrofitted sensors, has been the face of the company since its Phoenix debut years ago. The Ojai — boxier, built from the ground up with Zeekr in China as a purpose-made robotaxi — was, until now, something closer to a pilot program: a curiosity ferrying a lucky few. Making it fully public, and putting it to work earning fares, suggests Waymo has decided the minivan isn't a side project. It's the shape of the fleet to come.

That matters for a few reasons. First, the economics: a vehicle designed from scratch for autonomy, without a steering column or a driver's seat to route sensors around, is cheaper to build at scale than a retrofitted luxury SUV. If Waymo is ready to trust the Ojai with paying customers across multiple markets, it's a signal the company believes the manufacturing and safety case is solid enough to stop treating the vehicle as an experiment.

Second, the Gemini integration points to where the robotaxi business is actually competing now. The driving itself — the part that once seemed like the whole problem — is becoming table stakes. What's left to differentiate one robotaxi ride from another is the experience inside the box: whether the car can answer a question about where it's going, adjust a plan on the fly, or just make the ride feel less like being cargo. Google folding its flagship AI into Waymo's cars is also a reminder of how much this business depends on Alphabet's broader ambitions — the robotaxi isn't just a car, it's a rolling showcase for Gemini.

For riders, the change is mostly invisible until it isn't: fewer people stuck on a waitlist, more minivans on the street, and a back seat that might now offer conversation instead of silence. For the industry, it's a marker of maturity. Robotaxis have spent years proving they can drive. The Ojai's promotion from curiosity to workhorse suggests the next argument Waymo wants to win is about scale, cost, and what a ride ought to feel like — the unglamorous, decisive part of building a business that lasts.
