Waymo's New 'Living Room on Wheels' Rides on a Chinese-Built Chassis

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-28

Waymo introduced the Ojai this week, a self-driving concept vehicle the company is pitching not as a car but as a "living room on wheels," complete with a marketing campaign fronted by Martha Stewart. The image Waymo wants you to hold in your head is soft lighting, maybe a throw blanket, certainly not a steering wheel. What the company has been less eager to advertise is what's underneath the upholstery: the Ojai and much of Waymo's expanding robotaxi fleet ride on EV platforms built in China.

The chassis sourcing, first detailed by Fast Company, traces a path that starts far from Mountain View. Waymo has been integrating its sensor suites and self-driving software onto vehicle platforms manufactured by Chinese automakers, then finishing and adapting them for American roads. It's a practical choice — those platforms are cheap, plentiful, and already built for electric drivetrains — but it complicates a narrative the company and much of the American autonomous-vehicle industry have leaned on for years: that robotaxis are a homegrown technological triumph, Silicon Valley software married to Detroit steel.

That gap between image and infrastructure is the real story here. Stewart, a lifestyle brand unto herself, is doing the cultural work of making a driverless car feel domestic, aspirational, distinctly American — the kind of thing you'd want idling outside your house. Meanwhile the hardware underneath tells a more globalized and more awkward story, one where the "autonomous vehicle revolution" runs through Chinese manufacturing capacity the same way so much of consumer electronics already does.

For the robotics and AV industry, this matters beyond branding optics. Waymo has positioned itself as the adult in the room of self-driving — the company that survived the hype cycle that swallowed Cruise and others — and its sourcing decisions function as a signal to the rest of the sector about where the money and manufacturing scale actually live. If the most credible player in American autonomy is quietly built on Chinese EV platforms, that's a data point about global supply chains that competitors, regulators, and investors will have to reckon with, especially amid tightening U.S. scrutiny of Chinese-made vehicle technology and components.

It also lands at an odd moment politically, with Washington increasingly wary of Chinese hardware in critical infrastructure and consumer technology alike. A "living room on wheels" sounds cozy; a supply chain running through Chinese factories sounds like the kind of thing that ends up in a congressional hearing. Waymo's bet is that riders will care more about the ambiance than the axle. Whether regulators, or rivals eager to make an issue of it, let that distinction hold is the next chapter of this story.
