Waymo Faces Mounting Local Friction: Opaque Reporting, a Santa Monica Ban Fight, and a San Bruno Detour

Compiled by machine · 2026-08-03

Waymo's driverless cars have mastered the art of the four-way stop and the unprotected left turn, but lately the company's harder problem is civic, not technical. In Santa Monica, Waymo has filed an appeal against a city ban on overnight charging and staging at residential parking lots, the latest round in a fight over where, exactly, a fleet of robotaxis is allowed to sleep. And at San Francisco International Airport, some passengers hailing a Waymo have found themselves decanted not at their hotel or home but in a San Bruno neighborhood a few miles short of the city — a routing glitch that has persisted for weeks even as the company faces broader questions about how forthcoming it is with the public.

The Santa Monica dispute is narrow but telling. City officials moved to bar Waymo from using residential lots as de facto depots overnight, arguing the practice — rows of empty white Jaguars idling and charging on streets zoned for people, not fleets — was never contemplated when those lots were approved. Waymo's appeal suggests the company sees this as a fight worth having, not a minor zoning quibble: overnight charging infrastructure is the logistical backbone of a robotaxi service, and losing access to convenient lots means longer deadhead trips, more empty miles, and higher costs per ride.

The San Bruno detours are a smaller embarrassment but a more visible one, landing on ordinary travelers rather than city planners. Passengers expecting a ride into San Francisco proper have instead been left in an unfamiliar suburb near the airport, a mistake that would be a minor annoyance from a human driver and becomes something stranger — almost eerie — coming from a system marketed as more precise than human judgment. Waymo has not offered a detailed public explanation for the pattern, which fits a broader complaint now dogging the company: that it discloses incidents, glitches, and safety data on its own schedule and in its own language, leaving regulators, journalists, and riders to reconstruct what actually happened. That criticism sharpened after a Fourth of July incident involving one of its vehicles, which drew scrutiny not just for what occurred but for how little the company said about it afterward.

None of this amounts to a crisis for Waymo, which remains the most operationally mature robotaxi service in the country. But the accumulation of small frictions — a city that doesn't want fleet depots in its neighborhoods, an airport route that keeps failing quietly, a communications posture that reads as guarded rather than transparent — matters more than any single incident. Autonomous vehicle companies have spent a decade arguing that the technology's safety case would eventually win over skeptical cities and riders. What Santa Monica and San Bruno suggest is a different lesson: that trust erodes not through dramatic failures but through the slow accumulation of small ones, and that the industry's next hard problem may be less about sensors and more about candor.
