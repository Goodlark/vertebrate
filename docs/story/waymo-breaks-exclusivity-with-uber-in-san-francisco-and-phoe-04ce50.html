Waymo Breaks Exclusivity with Uber in San Francisco and Phoenix

Compiled by machine · 2026-11-04

Waymo is ending its exclusive robotaxi arrangement with Uber in San Francisco and Phoenix, the company confirmed this week, a decision that opens both markets to rival ride-hailing platforms and marks a notable fracture in one of the autonomous-vehicle industry's highest-profile partnerships. The change means Waymo's self-driving fleet will no longer be booked solely through Uber's app in those two cities, and Uber, in turn, is free to bring in competing robotaxi operators.

Waymo co-CEO Tekedra Mawakana didn't dress up the reasoning in diplomatic language. She described the Uber app integration as a "crapshoot" for riders, a phrase that suggests less a strategic pivot than a breakup over broken trust. Her comments implied that Uber, in building out the driverless experience inside its app, had prioritized speed of rollout over the basic reliability passengers expect — inconsistent pickups, confusing handoffs, the kind of friction that erodes confidence in a technology still trying to prove itself safe and seamless.

The split matters because it reveals how fragile the current architecture of the robotaxi business really is. Waymo, which operates its own fleet and owns the technology stack bumper to bumter, has leaned on partners like Uber to solve the harder consumer problem: getting a critical mass of riders to open an app and request a ride without friction. That arrangement made sense when Waymo was still scaling and needed distribution. Now, with Waymo running robust standalone operations in both San Francisco and Phoenix — two of its most mature markets — the company evidently feels it no longer needs Uber's demand funnel, and may see more upside in controlling the full rider experience itself.

For Uber, the loss of exclusivity is a reminder that its robotaxi strategy depends on goodwill from partners who increasingly have leverage. Uber has pitched itself as the neutral marketplace for autonomous vehicles, stitching together deals with Waymo, Nuro, and others rather than building its own self-driving cars. But that model only works if the AV companies need Uber more than Uber needs them. In two of the country's most closely watched autonomous-vehicle markets, Waymo just signaled it doesn't.

The broader stakes extend beyond corporate rivalry. Robotaxis are still a novelty to most Americans, and public trust is the industry's scarcest resource — one bad pickup, one stranded rider, one viral complaint can set back the narrative that driverless cars are ready for prime time. Mawakana's blunt critique suggests Waymo is increasingly willing to protect its brand by walking away from partners it believes are cutting corners, even lucrative ones. As more cities open up to autonomous fleets, that willingness to go it alone could become a template, or a warning, for how these alliances evolve next.
