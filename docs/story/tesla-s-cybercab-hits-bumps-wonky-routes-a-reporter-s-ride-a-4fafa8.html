Tesla's Cybercab Hits Bumps: Wonky Routes, a Reporter's Ride, and a Federal Inquiry

Compiled by machine · 2026-09-16

In Austin this month, Tesla's Cybercab has been giving riders a lesson in patience. Passengers hailing the two-seat, steering-wheel-less robotaxi for short neighborhood trips have found themselves on routes so circuitous that a ten-minute errand stretches into an hour-long tour of the city's back streets, according to Futurism, which documented multiple rides where the vehicle's software seemed to choose the scenic route by way of some inscrutable logic. An Axios reporter who booked a ride came away with little more clarity, describing the experience without being able to say much about how the car performs, when it might become a real commercial service, or what, exactly, Tesla is testing for. And on the regulatory side, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has now formally ordered Tesla to explain how the Cybercab meets federal safety certification standards — a request for technical documentation, not an accusation, but one that puts the vehicle's paperwork under a spotlight just as its public rollout stumbles.

Taken together, these three threads — bad routing, an unilluminating ride-along, and a federal inquiry — describe a company that has moved a highly futuristic-looking product into public streets before it has fully worked out the unglamorous plumbing underneath: mapping, routing algorithms, and the regulatory dossier that proves the car is safe to operate without a human backup driver. Cybercab has no steering wheel or pedals, a design choice that only works if the software is trustworthy enough to make the removal of manual controls a non-issue. Right now, the routing complaints suggest the software is still guessing.

The stakes here go beyond one city's traffic patterns. Tesla has staked enormous credibility, and a good chunk of its valuation story, on the idea that autonomy is close and that Cybercab represents a leap past incremental robotaxi rollouts from Waymo and others. Circuitous routes are an embarrassment; a federal certification inquiry is a different order of problem, because it asks Tesla to show its work on the very question — is this vehicle legally and technically qualified to carry passengers without human controls — that underpins the entire business model. If NHTSA isn't satisfied with the answers, the timeline for expanding Cybercab beyond Austin could slip further.

For riders, the effect so far is more inconvenience than danger: nobody's been hurt, but the promise of a driverless car that gets you somewhere faster than you could yourself is being undercut by rides that take four or five times longer than a human driver would need. For the wider industry, it's a reminder that the hard part of robotaxis was never building a car without a steering wheel — it was teaching the software underneath to make sound, boring, everyday decisions, thousands of times a day, without a regulator or a reporter noticing that something's off.
